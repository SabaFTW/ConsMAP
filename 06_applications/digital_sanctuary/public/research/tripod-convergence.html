The Tripod of Equilibrium: a dimensional framework for Abrahamic convergence Three religions, one truth, and the politics of limited sight. The “Tripod of Equilibrium”

proposes that Christianity, Judaism, and Islam are not competing truth-claims but different two-dimensional projections of the same higher-dimensional divine reality — much as a cylinder viewed from one angle appears as a circle and from another as a rectangle. This framework has no direct scholarly precedent under that name, but it converges remarkably with several major philosophical and theological traditions: John Hick’s pluralistic hypothesis, Frithjof Schuon’s transcendent unity of religions, Jain anekāntavāda, Plato’s Cave allegory, and Edwin Abbott’s Flatland. What makes the Tripod distinctive is its synthesis — combining geometric epistemology with a power-analysis of who benefits when observers mistake their partial projection for the whole object. The evidence gathered here suggests the geometric analogy is not merely a metaphor but a structurally sound epistemological framework, one that exposes how institutional actors have historically exploited perspective gaps to manufacture conflict. This report examines each dimension of the framework, distinguishing rigorously between what is historically verified, what is contested, and what remains speculative.

The projection metaphor has deep philosophical roots The core analogy — a single three-dimensional object generating contradictory two- dimensional views — is not new to philosophy, though applying it specifically to Abrahamic interfaith understanding is original. The metaphor’s intellectual lineage runs through at least 2,500 years of human thought.

Plato’s Allegory of the Cave (c. 375 BCE) is the foundational Western text. Prisoners chained in a cave see only shadows on a wall Wikipedia — two-dimensional projections of three-dimensional objects carried past a fire. The freed prisoner’s ascent into sunlight parallels the philosophical or spiritual journey from appearance to reality. Augustine equated Plato’s Form of the Good with God; the cave wall functions as the lower-dimensional screen on which reality is projected. The structural parallel to the Tripod is exact: shadows are 2D projections of a higher reality, and the prisoners who argue about shadow-shapes are arguing about perspectives, not about truth itself.

Edwin Abbott’s Flatland (1884) extends this into mathematical rigor. Abbott — an Anglican priest and headmaster — describes a two-dimensional world Wikipedia where a visiting Sphere appears as a constantly changing circle. A Square, the protagonist, cannot comprehend three dimensions until lifted out of his plane entirely. Wikipedia As mathematician Thomas Banchoff has argued, Abbott’s “metaphysical dimension is his main interest. He is trying to tell us something about our human failure to grasp higher realities.”

Brown University The analogy maps directly onto the Tripod: each Abrahamic tradition inhabits its own “Flatland,” perceiving cross-sections of a reality it cannot fully apprehend from within its plane.

Carl Sagan’s 1980 Cosmos demonstration brought this to mass audiences — dipping an apple in ink to show how a 3D object creates ever-changing 2D impressions. Multiple commentators noted the irony that Sagan, a committed skeptic, provided one of the most powerful theological analogies of the twentieth century: Create-A-Way! a higher- dimensional being whose true nature is incomprehensible to lower-dimensional creatures, who perceive only “cross-sections” of divine reality.

Immanuel Kant’s phenomenal/noumenal distinction (1781) supplies the philosophical backbone. The noumenal world — things-in-themselves — contains more reality than our perceptual apparatus can process. Our cognitive categories act as “projection operators”

that reduce full noumenal reality to phenomenal appearance. What appears as a circle from one angle and a rectangle from another is unified in the higher-dimensional object. This is structurally identical to the Tripod of Equilibrium. The paradox maps precisely onto Nietzsche’s perspectivism, the most rigorous philosophical defense of perspective-dependent knowledge. In On the Genealogy of Morality (1887), Nietzsche wrote the definitive statement: “There is only a perspective seeing, only a perspective ‘knowing’; and the more affects we allow to speak about one thing, the more eyes, different eyes, we can use to observe one thing, the more complete will our ‘concept’ of this thing, our ‘objectivity,’ be.” A-Z Quotes Crucially, perspectivism is not relativism — Nietzsche does not claim all perspectives are equally true, only that none achieves the “view from nowhere.” Wikipedia The Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda (“many-sidedness”), dating to approximately 500 BCE, is the oldest formal system addressing this paradox. Toward a complete Tripod: what the geometry reveals The Tripod of Equilibrium, synthesized from all available evidence, functions as three nested arguments of decreasing certainty.

The first and strongest argument (HIGH confidence) is epistemological: limited observers necessarily produce partial, perspective-dependent accounts of any reality that exceeds their dimensional access. This is established across Platonic philosophy, Kantian epistemology, Wittgensteinian aspect-seeing, Jain anekāntavāda, and formal perspectivism.

Applied to the Abrahamic religions, the documented historical evidence overwhelmingly supports that Christianity, Judaism, and Islam share common theological architecture, common source figures, and common ethical frameworks, while their differences are systematically explicable through the cultural and historical contexts in which each emerged. The cylinder analogy is structurally sound: each tradition captures genuine features of a shared divine reality while necessarily missing features visible only from other angles.

The second argument (MEDIUM confidence) is historical-political: institutional actors have repeatedly exploited perspective gaps between religions for purposes of power consolidation. The Crusades provide the clearest documented case — a papacy seeking to heal a schism and consolidate authority channeled genuine religious devotion into campaigns whose atrocities were driven by dehumanizing rhetoric that constructed the “infidel” as sub-human. Contemporary parallels are documented in the rise of Christian nationalism, the instrumentalization of antisemitism from multiple political directions, and the construction of “Islamisation” narratives that distort demographic data. The scholarly framework of moral panics (Cohen, Hall, Critcher) provides rigorous mechanisms for understanding how these manufactured perspective conflicts function. This argument does not require a supernatural “4D observer” — institutional power, media amplification, and political incentive structures are sufficient explanatory mechanisms.
