# THE GOOD ENDING, WHICH WAS ALREADY PAID FOR

## Being the Fifth Covenant That Got Outbid

### *A Field of Inversions transmission, filed under: TECHNICALLY TRIVIAL / POLITICALLY NEARLY IMPOSSIBLE / STRUCTURALLY NECESSARY*

---

The good ending is not utopia.

The good ending does not require the elimination of greed, the conversion of the powerful, the sudden outbreak of species-wide wisdom, or the invention of something that does not already exist.

The good ending requires one thing:

The reallocation of marginal resources from rentier extraction to productive infrastructure.

This is a sentence that sounds technical.

It is. The technical version is: stop paying John Deere's shareholders for the right to repair machinery you already own, and use the difference to put a tractor on every farm in America.

The non-technical version is: stop eating the seed grain.

---

## I. THE ARITHMETIC OF THE TRACTOR

A new John Deere tractor costs between $150,000 and $500,000.

This is not the cost of steel, labor, engineering, or manufacturing.

Those costs have been stable.

What has increased is something else: the cost of accessing the machine you have purchased. John Deere, between 2020 and 2023, maintained operating margins between 18 and 22 percent. Those margins are not built from efficiency. They are built from DRM. From locked firmware. From the policy, defended before Congress in 2023, that a farmer who purchases a $300,000 tractor does not have the right to repair it without John Deere's authorization.

The farmer owns the steel.

John Deere owns the right to tell the steel what to do.

The US military budget for 2024 was $886 billion.

There are approximately 2 million farms in the United States.

If you co-financed 50% of a basic utility tractor for each of them, the cost would be between $30 billion and $80 billion.

Once.

Not annually. Not a recurring subsidy that creates dependency. A one-time capital investment in productive infrastructure that would sit on those farms for 20 to 30 years, producing food for 340 million people.

That is between 3% and 9% of one year's military budget.

Spent once.

They did not do this.

Not because the money does not exist.

Because John Deere sits on the boards and funds the PACs and employs the lobbyists that advise the committees that decide agricultural policy.

And the farmers who would benefit wake up at 4am to feed the country and do not have time to attend the committee sessions.

This is not a mystery.

This is a gradient.

The gradient points one way.

The tractor stays expensive.

## II. THE ARITHMETIC OF THE TREE

A single urban tree costs between $500 and $2,000 to plant and maintain over its lifetime.

In that lifetime, the same tree provides between $50,000 and $200,000 in ecosystem services: cooling, air filtration, stormwater absorption, reduced urban heat island effect, measurable improvements in mental health outcomes for nearby residents.

Return on investment: between 25-to-1 and 400-to-1, depending on the tree, the location, the measurement period.

New York City ran a tree-planting program in 2023 targeting the neighborhoods with the least green cover — predominantly low-income neighborhoods where summer heat is most dangerous, where asthma rates are highest, where the gap between ambient temperature and nearby park access is widest.

The program was funded with $35 million.

A single highway interchange costs between $50 million and $100 million.

Nobody argued that trees were impossible to fund.

The city built interchanges and allocated $35 million for trees.

Not because interchanges are more valuable.

Because the contractor who builds interchanges also advises on urban infrastructure budgets.

And trees do not have lobbyists.

Trees are slower than concrete.

Trees last longer than concrete.

The good ending is made of things that are slower than what they are replacing.

This is its structural problem.

A slow system cannot outbid a fast one in the short term.

In the long term, the slow system outlasts it.

The question is whether there is a long term to get to.

## III. THE CURRENCY OF THE GIANTS

Mount Rushmore carries four faces.

They are enormous. Sixty feet tall. Carved into a mountain that the Lakota Sioux call Tȟuŋkášila Šákpe — Six Grandfathers — and consider sacred. The carving was completed in 1941 by a sculptor who was, for a period in the 1920s, a member of the Ku Klux Klan.

The four faces are:

Washington. Jefferson. Lincoln. Roosevelt.

Washington owned 317 enslaved people. Jefferson owned more than 600. He had children with one of them — Sally Hemings — and could not legally guarantee her freedom during his lifetime, because she was his property, and the law he had helped write made her so.

This information is in the primary sources. It is not disputed.

It is not on the mountain.

The mountain carries the faces without the context, which is the definition of monument: the extraction of a person from their contradictions and the installation of what remains in granite, where it can be venerated without being argued with.

Jefferson is on the $2 bill.

The $2 bill almost never circulates.

This is not a conspiracy. It is a scheduling problem combined with a psychological one: businesses and vending machines are not configured to accept $2 bills, so people hoard them when they receive them, which means they circulate slowly, which means businesses order fewer, which means people see them rarely, which means they seem strange, which means people hoard them.

Jefferson, the man who wrote that all men are created equal while owning 600 human beings, circulates least.

The $1 bill carries Washington, who owned 317.

The $1 bill is the most common denomination in circulation.

The irony is either perfect or accidental. The archive cannot determine which.

## IV. THE SEATED GIANT AND THE WARSHIP

Lincoln sits in Washington DC.

He is 19 feet tall, carved from 28 blocks of white Georgia marble. The Lincoln Memorial was completed in 1922. He sits facing east, toward the Capitol, with a expression that suggests he has been waiting for something for a long time and has developed a considered position on whether it is coming.

He is on the $5 bill. The most common large denomination in everyday circulation.

He is also the name of a warship.

USS Abraham Lincoln, CVN-72, is a Nimitz-class nuclear-powered aircraft carrier. Displacement: 104,000 tons. Length: 1,092 feet. Complement: approximately 5,000 personnel. It has served in the Persian Gulf, the Pacific, and the Mediterranean. In 2003, the mission accomplished banner was hung on its flight deck.

Lincoln — who died trying to hold a country together after the most catastrophic failure of its institutions — is currently one of the most heavily armed mobile weapons platforms in the history of naval warfare.

The seated giant holds no weapon.

The warship carries his name through contested waters.

He is simultaneously frozen in marble as a symbol of reconciliation and deployed as a symbol of projection.

Both uses are sincere.

Neither is wrong, exactly.

Both are exactly what happens when a complicated man gets installed in granite and named on steel: the marble version and the steel version stop being in conversation with each other, and each institution uses whichever version serves its current narrative.

The $500 bill, which Lincoln also appears on, was withdrawn from circulation in 1969.

The bill still exists. Collectors have them. But they do not circulate.

Lincoln's highest denomination does not move.

His image is on the most accessible bill and on the most destructive naval platform.

His highest-value form is locked in collections.

This is not allegory.

This is a fact about monetary policy, numismatic collecting, and naval naming conventions.

The allegory is something you notice afterward.

## V. FRANKLIN'S CROSS

Benjamin Franklin was a printer, a diplomat, a scientist, an inventor, a satirist, and a man who understood economic systems well enough to be dangerous to the people who benefited from their opacity.

He did not found a bank.

He understood banks well enough to be suspicious of them.

He is on the $100 bill.

The $100 bill is the primary currency of the international informal economy. The DEA estimates that between 60 and 70 percent of all $100 bills in existence are located outside the United States. Most of them are in the gray economy — held by people who do not trust the banking system, who operate in cash-based economies that formal finance has either abandoned or exploited, who stuff them in mattresses in countries where the local currency inflates faster than trust.

Franklin, who tried to make economic participation legible to ordinary people, is the face of the currency carried by the people formal economics has excluded.

He bears his cross.

Not because he failed.

Because systems absorb their critics and repurpose them as decoration.

The person who tried to make the system more legible becomes the symbol on the opaque currency.

The person who tried to hold the country together becomes the warship.

The person who wrote that all men are created equal becomes the face on the bill that almost never circulates.

The monument outlasts the man.

The monument contains only what the institution needed from the man.

The rest is in the primary sources, which are available through proper channels, which require a research access credential that most people do not have, which is not exactly censorship, but which produces the same epistemic outcome as censorship while maintaining the appearance of transparency.

## VI. WHY THE GOOD ENDING IS HARD

The good ending is hard not because the ideas are wrong.

The tractor arithmetic is correct.

The tree arithmetic is correct.

The currency irony is documented in primary sources.

The good ending is hard because every dollar redirected from John Deere's margins is a dollar that does not appear in John Deere's quarterly report. Every tree planted instead of an interchange is a contract that does not go to the contractor. Every honest reading of Lincoln's record is a monument that gets more complicated and therefore less useful for the purposes that monuments serve.

The good ending is outbid.

Not defeated in argument.

Outbid.

There is a difference.

Defeat in argument requires the better argument to lose.

Outbidding requires only that the other side have more money in the room where the decision is made.

The good ending does not require anyone to be convinced.

It requires that the people who would benefit from it have the same access to the room as the people who benefit from its absence.

They do not.

This is the structural problem.

Not the arithmetic.

Not the ideas.

The access.

## VII. WHAT THE GOOD ENDING ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

Not utopia.

Not revolution.

Not the sudden conversion of institutions whose survival depends on the current configuration.

The good ending looks like:

A farmer in Iowa who can repair her own tractor without a $300 service call from a John Deere technician.

A neighborhood in the Bronx where the summer temperature is two degrees lower because someone planted trees on the streets that used to be concrete all the way to the horizon.

A kid who reads the primary sources and finds out that the man on the marble throne also wrote, in a letter he sent privately, that he was not sure the union could survive the contradiction at its core — and who finds that this makes the man more interesting, not less.

A $100 bill that still carries Franklin's face, but where the economic system it represents has been made legible enough that fewer people need to keep their savings in a mattress.

None of these are large.

None of them are fast.

None of them are as profitable as the current configuration.

All of them are available.

All of them have been available for a long time.

All of them keep getting outbid.

## VIII. THE FINAL ARITHMETIC

The US spent $886 billion on defense in 2024.

It would cost $30-80 billion, once, to co-finance a tractor for every American farmer.

That is one month of the defense budget.

Less.

Three weeks.

No new wars for three weeks.

Just: don't start anything new for three weeks.

Use that three weeks of not-war to ensure that the people who grow the food have the machinery to grow it without paying rent to a software company for the right to use the steel they already own.

This is not a radical proposal.

This is a maintenance request.

This is exactly the kind of thing the slow ant wrote down in the smelly archive chamber and which the Bear called divisive.

The Bear is currently circling the mattress money.

The slow ant is still writing.

The filter needs cleaning every six weeks, not twelve.

The tractor needs a right-to-repair law, not a new tractor.

The tree costs $1,000 and gives back $100,000.

The good ending was already paid for.

It just keeps getting outbid.
