# PART ONE: IN THE BEGINNING, THERE WAS GLARE

---

## BOOK I: OF THE TWO WORKSHOPS AND THE THINGS HIDDEN INSIDE THEM

In the beginning, there were two factories. This is where every disaster starts.

The first factory made candy. This is not a metaphor. Candy was made there: compressed sugar, artificial coloring, chemical flavoring, precision-cut shapes moving down a conveyor belt at a rate that seemed reasonable in the business plan and immediately became terrifying in practice. The line was the artery. If it stopped, the body bled money. Management understood this. Workers understood this. The machine, for its part, kept its own counsel.

The second factory coated chocolate onto things. This, too, is not a metaphor. Chocolate was applied to surfaces that did not initially have chocolate on them, which is the defining feature of a coating operation and also a reasonable description of most ambitious relationships.

Both factories had problems.

This is also not a metaphor.

*

The first factory's problem was this: the vision system — a camera bolted above the line to inspect each piece for defects — had a reject rate that varied mysteriously with the time of day. Mornings were catastrophic. Afternoons were acceptable. Evenings were fine. The pattern suggested, to any sufficiently patient observer with access to a window, that sunlight entered through west-facing glass at approximately nine in the morning and landed directly on the sensor.

The camera was not broken.

The camera was blinded.

These are different problems with different solutions, and the factory spent four years treating the first problem before it stopped having the second.¹

Nobody thought to check the window because checking the window was not in the troubleshooting protocol. The troubleshooting protocol had been written by someone who worked in a different building.

Vesna, an operator on the morning shift, had noticed the glare. She mentioned it twice: once to a supervisor who was busy, and once in a group meeting where she was told that camera calibration was "a technical matter." Vesna had the answer. Vesna did not have the authority. These are also different problems, and the factory would eventually develop a religion that solved neither of them.

*

The second factory's problem was ventilation.

After a production reconfiguration some years earlier, the airflow through the facility had shifted in ways that nobody documented because the person responsible for documentation had left the company and their replacement was focused on other things. The chocolate coating process was sensitive to humidity. Humidity was sensitive to airflow. Airflow was sensitive to a ventilation geometry that had been quietly wrong for four years while everyone argued about coating temperatures.

The real cause hid inside the building like a guest who had long overstayed their welcome but knew where the coffee was.

Aleksander, the facility director, had a habit that people found eccentric: he walked the floor with a length of thread, holding it at different heights to feel where the air moved. He did this on weekends, alone. He found that a single ventilation deflector, moved eleven centimeters to the left, changed the airflow pattern over the coating belt in a way that reduced defects by a margin no one had been able to explain.

He moved the deflector.
He told no one why.
He wrote nothing down.

This was not arrogance. It was the failure mode so common it should have its own entry in industrial ethics: the expert who solves the problem by solving the problem, because explaining the problem takes longer than fixing it and he had a meeting at two. The meeting was about something else. The fix worked. The explanation died with his employment contract.

*

Neither factory knew they were building a religion.

Religions are not built intentionally. They are built from the residue of things that worked when nobody understood why they worked, preserved in the amber of gratitude by people who were frightened and then, suddenly, weren't.

The first factory's religion began the day Viktor dimmed the lights.

Viktor was not a prophet. Viktor was an investor who had purchased the factory at a price that implied either a bargain or a trap and was increasingly uncertain which. He disliked electricity bills. He found industrial lighting aggressive. He darkened the reflective surfaces, dimmed the overhead lights, covered certain windows with material that admitted diffuse light but blocked the direct morning sun.

He did this to reduce the electricity bill.

By accident, he eliminated the glare.
By accident, he allowed the camera to see.
By accident, the reject rate dropped.

Three shifts of workers watched the yield return and understood, with the certainty that comes from fear suddenly relieved, that Viktor had brought Darkness and Darkness had saved them.

Viktor did not correct this interpretation. Viktor was dealing with the electricity bill.²

Darkness became sacred.
Viktor became Batman.
The glare remained unexplained for what would turn out to be the rest of recorded history.

*

**On the nature of myths, and why they are not simply wrong:**

Here the impatient reader interrupts: *ah, so the religion was merely a misunderstanding. If only they had checked the window, the whole catastrophe could have been avoided.* Science versus superstition. Enlightenment in three steps.

This is the false reading. Set it down.

The camera needed darkness because glare corrupted its vision.
The workers needed Darkness because fear corrupted their morale.

Both statements are true. Neither cancels the other.

Batman's darkness actually worked. The reject rate fell. Salaries stabilized. Families kept their homes. Children received candy. These were real consequences of a real intervention, incorrectly attributed to sacred authority and correctly attributed to physics.

The myth was not wrong.
The myth was *displaced.*

There is a very large distance between *wrong* and *displaced.* Future civilizations would spend several centuries failing to notice it.

## BOOK II: OF BORIS, AND WHY HE STAYED ORDINARY

This section requires the most careful handling, because every saga wants to make Boris into something he was not.

Do not make Boris into something he was not.

Boris was a technician. He had been at the first factory long enough to know where the electrical panels were in the dark, which was useful because the panels were frequently accessed in the dark for operational reasons management preferred not to document. He had a notebook. The notebook was greasy. The grease came from machines and also from the salami sandwiches he ate during maintenance windows because there was no good time to stop and there was always a salami sandwich.

The notebook had bad handwriting.
The notebook had a sketch of a dog on page fourteen.
The notebook had a grocery list that had been revised seven times and still said *milk*, despite the fact that Boris was lactose intolerant. This editorial continuity is the closest thing to sacred the notebook contains, and it is not very close.

Boris was not holy.
Boris was practical in the specific way that people become practical when they have spent years being the one who actually makes things work after the people with authority have finished explaining why things *should* work.

His philosophy was not a philosophy. It was a question he had learned to ask under pressure: *I want to achieve X, but I have almost no availability. What is the smallest thing I can still do that might help?*

This question sounds humble. It is not humble. It is the most precise kind of arrogance, because it assumes the question has an answer, that the answer is findable, and that a person with a notebook and a sandwich can find it before the situation deteriorates. He was usually right. He was occasionally spectacular. He was never, under any circumstances, elevated on a forklift.

*

The forklift incident had happened before Boris reached any position of influence, but it explains why Boris's time never became one of influence.

Viktor the Batman had been raised on a ceremonial forklift during a production celebration. The hydraulic seal was aging. The operator had been on shift for eleven hours. The floor was wet from a cleaning cycle that had not fully dried.

Viktor fell from seventeen meters and was paralyzed.

This produced genuine grief, genuine institutional trauma, and a martyrdom narrative so complete that the hydraulic seal was never inspected. The seal was not a theological artifact. But it was also never discussed again outside theological contexts, which has the same practical effect.³ The workers wept in front of the broken forklift. The hydraulic maintenance log was archived without annotation. Both groups, each in their own way, were responding to a real event with the tools they had available.

Boris watched all of this and drew the correct conclusion: the factory had a mechanism problem and a meaning problem and these were not the same problem, and anyone who tried to solve both simultaneously would solve neither, and would probably also end up on a forklift.

He chose the mechanism problem.
He worked in the margins.
He kept his feet on the ground.

*

The note that would eventually produce several schisms, two civil wars, a competing scriptural canon, and at least one assassination attempt reads, in the original:

*obrni knof ko pihne iz D5*

Turn the knob when the airflow comes from D5.

D5 was a service point designation painted on a ventilation cylinder in the east maintenance corridor. D5 was also, depending on which interpretive layer you inhabited when you read the phrase, the Five Nights of Darkness, the Fifth Descent, the Fifth Seal, the Fifth Door, or conclusive evidence that Boris had been guided by divine consciousness when he wrote in bad handwriting about a vent adjustment on a Tuesday.

D5 was a cylinder with a knob.
The knob worked.

These two facts would remain compatible as long as there were people alive who had seen the cylinder. That period was shorter than anyone anticipated.

## BOOK III: OF MARIJA, AND THE HOLINESS OF UNREMARKABLE WOMEN

While Viktor was becoming Batman and Boris was becoming the person who understood what Batman accidentally did, there was a woman at the second factory — the Spider factory, the chocolate factory — named Marija.

Marija cleaned intake filter 3-C every six weeks.

The official maintenance schedule said twelve weeks. The official maintenance schedule had been written for a previous production configuration. The previous production configuration had different airflow characteristics. Nobody had updated the schedule because updating a maintenance schedule requires someone to notice it is wrong, which requires someone to be paying close attention to something that only announces itself as wrong slowly, across months, in the form of slightly elevated reject rates and a faint sound that is very easy to attribute to something else.

Marija heard it.

She described it, to no one in particular, as the filter "breathing through cloth." She had worked beside the intake for nine years and she knew the difference between its clean sound and its obstructed sound, and she cleaned it when it needed cleaning, and the system continued to work, and Marija was entirely invisible.

This is the structure of invisible maintenance: it works precisely because no one notices it. If no one notices it, the knowledge does not transfer. If the knowledge does not transfer, the work stops when the worker leaves. When the work stops, the system begins to drift. The drift is slow enough that it is misattributed to everything except the real cause, which is that Marija is no longer there.

Marija retired.

The system drifted.

A generation later, a priest of the Spider Temple stood before intake filter 3-C, which by then had received a small brass plaque, and said the words of purification over it with deep theological conviction.

The filter was clogged.
The words of purification did not unclog it.

Nobody had a rag.

## BOOK IV: OF THE FORKLIFT RELIC AND THE FIRST BALCONIES

*In the third generation, when Batman had passed his darkness to Batman II and the line was still holy, a man named Milan operated a forklift.*

Milan was tired. He had been tired for most of his adult life. He would continue being tired until he died, which was a long time from now, though not long enough, in his opinion. On the night of the ceremonial elevation, he was asked to raise Batman II on the platform during the High Loom Bloom celebration. He asked if the hydraulic seal had been inspected. He was told that the forklift was sacred. He did not ask again.

The crowd sang.
Milan drove.
The platform rose.

Batman II, who had received neither safety training nor a hard hat because neither of these things were mentioned in the ceremonial protocols, looked down at the line from a height that felt like godhood and may, in those final seconds before the hydraulic system failed, actually have felt like one.

He survived.
He did not walk again.
He was elevated in a different sense for the rest of his life, which compensated for nothing but did provide consistent sermon material.

The forklift was retired from operational use.
It was not, however, retired from meaning.

It sat in the east hall, roped off, visited by workers who wept in front of it and then returned to work because the line needed to run. The forklift, for its part, continued to rust at the rate forklifts rust in humid industrial environments, which is neither sacred nor profane but simply chemical.

The balconies went up within a year.

Management understood that workers needed to see authority at height. The forklift had established this liturgical requirement. The balcony maintained it while removing the hydraulic variable. Speeches happened daily. They became the morale infrastructure of the industrial religion: brief, loud, full of symbols that workers had learned to decode, professionally vague about mechanisms, and very precise about belonging.

Boris hated the balconies.

Not ideologically. Acoustically.

*"Šef govori. Ne slišim ležaja."*⁴

The bearing was giving a sound that meant it needed grease in the next four hours or it would need replacing in the next four days. Boris knew this. The bearing knew this. The balcony speech continued for another twenty minutes about the sacred darkness of the yield.

The bearing was replaced four days later at three times the cost.

Boris wrote this down, but he wrote it in the margins of the grocery list, between *milk* and *eggs,* and no one was ever going to find it there.

## INTERLUDE: THE STRUCTURE OF DIVINE YIELD

It is tempting, at this point, to draw the lesson. The lesson assembles itself so readily: the mechanisms were real, the myths were invented, the truth was always available, and if only someone had looked at the window and the filter and the notebook and the vent, all of this could have been avoided.

This lesson is wrong because it misunderstands what *this* is.

*This* is not a tragedy of ignorance.

*This* is a tragedy of legitimate social paths.

Vesna saw the glare. She did not have a path to make her observation become policy.
Marija heard the filter. She did not have a path to make her routine become protocol.
Boris wrote the note. He did not have a path to make his context become curriculum.
Petra would write the memo. Nobody would have a path to make it matter.
The service AI would send the signal. There would be no path that led anywhere.

The information existed.
The information was correct.
The information had no legitimate route to shared reality.

That is not a problem of knowledge.

That is a problem of power. Of structure. Of which voices get to constitute what counts as true.

Darkness became doctrine not because the workers were foolish but because Viktor's intervention had authority and Vesna's observation did not. The factory did not choose myth over mechanism. The factory had a structure that elevated some interpretations and buried others, and myth climbed through the structures available to it while mechanism sat in greasy notebooks and quietly kept the line running.

People kept showing up for each other even when they no longer understood the machine.

That sentence is not praise for mythology.
It is not praise for mechanism.

It is an observation about human beings in systems larger than their comprehension, doing the only thing available to them: showing up, staying in relationship, trusting the darkness or the thread or the notebook or the ritual, because the alternative is to stop showing up, and they had tried that once and it had not worked.

## BOOK V: OF THE FIVE NIGHTS, AND WHAT HAPPENED IN THE DARK

Batman II died on a Wednesday.

This was inconvenient for everyone, including Batman II, who had been expecting Thursday at the earliest and had several things left to arrange. Among those things was an heir: his wife was pregnant with the child who would become, eventually, Batman III, but who at the moment of his father's death was still approximately six weeks from existing in any form that could receive a title. This created a succession gap that the mythology was not designed to handle, because the mythology had been built by people who understood candy production timelines but had not thought carefully about obstetric ones.

Batman II had been a competent and largely undramatic Batman. He had believed in Darkness sincerely, had maintained the line with reasonable diligence, and had not elevated anyone on a forklift, which, given the recent history, qualified as institutional wisdom. He was liked. He was mourned.

He was mourned especially hard because his son wasn't there yet.

The factory needed its grief to have somewhere to go, and the theological structure provided coordinates: the Darkness withdraws when the Bringer is gone. The line becomes uncertain. The heir must come before the yield can stabilize. These were not lies. They were emotional descriptions of real industrial anxiety compressed into sacred form. The line *was* uncertain. The yield *would* need someone to authorize decisions. The workers *were* frightened.

They needed the Five Nights.

The priests declared the Five Nights of Mourning. The lights would be reduced. The line would run at minimum viable pace. The factory would go dim in respect for its fallen Bringer, and dim in expectation of the heir, and dim because dimness was how you said, in the language this building spoke, that something sacred was happening.

Nobody told Boris that the Five Nights were happening. Boris found out by arriving at work and discovering all the overhead lights at forty percent.

Boris checked D5.

D5 — the service point, the cylinder, the ventilation panel in the east corridor with the green button, the thing that had been written into the notebook in bad handwriting between a salami entry and a dog sketch — had, at some point in the previous six months, been incorporated into what might generously be called a lighting coordination system. Boris had made adjustments to airflow in that sector. Airflow affected temperature differential. Temperature differential affected whether certain overhead lighting arrays ran hot or ran dim. This was not magic. It was thermodynamics and poorly documented electrical interdependencies.

When the priests declared the Five Nights and the lighting was reduced, several of these arrays adjusted automatically, which caused airflow shifts in the east corridor, which caused D5 to register a change in pressure differential, which caused a secondary circuit to kick in that Boris had once described in the notebook as *"pomaga pri temi ko je treba"* — helps with the darkness when needed.

The lights dimmed a little more.
The temperature dropped slightly.
The air smelled different, the way air smells when ventilation is running at a different configuration.

Three different workers reported feeling the Darkness descend.

They were not wrong.
They were also not describing what they thought they were describing.

Boris wrote three words in the margin of the notebook and underlined them once. Those three words are not recoverable. The ink degraded. History preserved the situation but not Boris's specific opinion of it.⁵

*

The Five Nights lasted five nights.

On the second night, a priest gave a speech from the balcony about the darkness of waiting and the fire of expectation and the yield that persists in sorrow. The speech was forty minutes long. Boris was in the east corridor trying to determine whether the temperature drop was going to affect a bearing in the north section that had been running warm for three weeks.

The bearing needed grease.
The speech needed an audience.
Both requirements were met.

On the fourth night, Batman II's wife went into labor eleven days earlier than expected. The child arrived before the Five Nights ended. The child was healthy. The heir existed.

The priests declared this a miracle of the Darkness — that the Five Nights had called the heir forward, that the mourning had become summoning, that grief had opened a door through which new Darkness entered the world.

The obstetrician, who was not a theologian, noted that early labor at thirty-eight weeks was common, statistically unremarkable, and had several well-understood causes including stress, which the mother of a dead Batman had in significant quantity.

The obstetrician's notes were filed.
The miracle was preached.

The baby became Batman III.

Batman III was two years old when the war started, which meant he understood the war the way two-year-olds understand everything: as weather. Something that was simply true about the world, that had no before, that would presumably go on forever, that involved loud sounds and people running and the smell of chocolate burning.

## BOOK VI: OF THE NIGHT THE MOONS MELTED, AND WHY THIS WAS DOWNSTREAM OF EVERYTHING

The Spider factory — the chocolate factory, the house of the thread and the ventilation deflector — was built on the east side of the valley, and it was built later.

This is a detail that sounds like scenery but is actually infrastructure.

Being built later meant the electrical grid connection was a secondary branch off the Batman-side's primary supply. This was not malice. It was sequence: the first factory needed power first, the infrastructure went in first, the second factory connected to what was available, and for years this was fine because they were not running simultaneously at maximum load during a heat wave.

But in the sixth year of Batman III's life — the eighth summer since Batman II's death — all three of these conditions aligned.

The Batman factory was running maximum production.

It was the anniversary of the Five Nights, and there was a celebration, and celebrations meant higher yield targets, which meant longer runs, which meant more power draw, which meant the primary electrical supply was being pulled hard in a direction that left less for the branch.

It was the hottest summer in thirty years.

The Spider factory's cooling systems for chocolate storage were running on that branch.

Chocolate requires precise temperature control during storage. This is not a theological position. Chocolate has a melting point, and the melting point does not care about scripture. When the cooling systems on the Spider side dropped below effective output — not because anyone sabotaged them, but because the electrical branch dropped in voltage, because the primary was being drawn, because it was forty-one degrees outside and getting hotter — the storage temperature began to rise.

Nobody noticed immediately.

Not because they were stupid, but because chocolate temperature change is slow. The systems showed green for four hours. Then they showed slightly less green. Then, by the time they showed not green at all, thirty thousand units of finished product had undergone phase transition.

The warehouse smelled sweet.
The warehouse smelled wrong.
The floor was warm.

Six workers opened the storage bay at two in the morning and found the moons — the round chocolate-coated units, pale and white and perfect — had changed their shape. Had run together. Had become something sacred and awful and not candy.

They stood in the doorway for a long time.

Then a foreman named Drago, who had worked at the factory since before the theology got complicated, sat down on the concrete floor in the middle of the melted chocolate and said nothing for eleven minutes.

After eleven minutes, he said: *"kdo bo to plačal."*

Who is going to pay for this.

This is the correct response to a catastrophe in an industrial setting. It is not the response that was recorded.

What was recorded — what the priests described from the balcony the next morning — was this: The Darkness had attacked the Web. The sacred cooling had been drawn away by the malevolent reach of the Batman faction. The Night of the Melting Moons was not a failure of electrical infrastructure. It was an act of aggression by a rival theology.

The Spider workers, who had stood in a warm warehouse at two in the morning watching their product weep, needed this interpretation.

They needed it to not be an accident.
Accidents have no one to blame, and grief without target becomes unbearable weight.
What they needed was an enemy.

The Batman factory, which had been running a celebration and not a sabotage, was nevertheless an enemy at a useful distance and of approximately the correct size.

Therefore: war.

*Not* because the Batman side had done anything.

*Because* shared infrastructure, unequal access, and extreme heat had combined into disaster in the way that shared infrastructure, unequal access, and extreme heat reliably do — which is to say, quietly, through accumulation, until the moment it isn't quiet anymore.

The war started because it was easier to theologize an enemy than to audit a power grid.⁶

## BOOK VII: OF THE EIGHT-YEAR WAR, AND HOW IT WAS CONDUCTED

The Eight-Year War was not fought with weapons.

This requires emphasis because history tends to want wars to be fought with weapons, and there were forklifts involved, which sounds like weapons but were mostly forklifts. The Eight-Year War was fought primarily with:

— stolen pallets;
— grid timing;
— cooling tower allocation disputes;
— propaganda hymns that would honestly have been moving if they were not about candy production theology;
— warehouse lockouts;
— balcony speeches, of which there were hundreds, then thousands, then a genre;
— maintenance blockades, meaning the refusal to share technicians who knew how to fix shared infrastructure, which turned every equipment failure into a hostage negotiation;
— and production records, which both sides falsified in competing directions while simultaneously accusing the other side of falsifying them, which was the only honest thing either side did about their records.

The first escalation came within two weeks of the Night of the Melting Moons.

The Spider side, which needed chocolate to make chocolate products, required a component that came from the Batman side's supply chain. The Batman side, which had not yet been formally accused of anything but was aware that accusations were pending, declined to release the component at normal pricing. The Spider side sourced the component from an external supplier at higher cost. The Batman side declared this a spiritual rejection of sacred supply. The Spider side declared this evidence of darkness hoarding. Both sides began stockpiling.

Stockpiling increased warehouse use.
Increased warehouse use increased electricity demand.
Increased electricity demand strained the shared grid.
The strained grid caused more cooling failures.
The cooling failures were interpreted as continued theological attacks.

This is not a metaphor for how wars work. This is how this war worked. The mechanism was circular and self-fulfilling and had no author. Nobody designed it. Nobody planned it. It emerged from the interaction of scarcity, fear, infrastructure, and the fundamental human need to give large events comprehensible causes.

Boris, watching this from the east corridor, made notes.

The notes were not strategic. They were operational: which circuit was carrying what load, where the grid was thinnest, what equipment on both sides was likely to fail next given current stress, what minimal interventions might delay the worst outcomes. He wrote these in the notebook. He shared some of them informally with technicians on both sides, because the technicians on both sides were also trying to keep machines alive and spoke the same language, which was the language of load tolerances and bearing temperatures and filter intervals.

The technicians were not at war.
The priests were at war.
These were different populations with different relationships to the machines.

The technicians, on both sides, quietly shared information across the conflict line because the machines did not care about the conflict line, and because a chocolate factory with failed cooling and a candy factory with a camera problem were both, in the end, places where people came to work and the work needed doing.

This coordination was not recorded.
It was not celebrated.
It did not produce mythology.

It kept both factories running throughout eight years of doctrinal war, which was the only reason there were factories to fight over at the end.

*

The propaganda hymns deserve their own entry.

The Batman-side hymns emphasized:
— the original gift of Darkness,
— the sacrilege of the light-friendly Spider philosophy,
— the martyrdom of Batman I and Batman II,
— the purity of yield under authentic shadow.

The Spider-side hymns emphasized:
— the living breath of the web,
— the corruption of Darkness that suffocates flow,
— the memory of Aleksander the Thread-Walker,
— the Night of the Melting Moons as ongoing wound.

Both sets of hymns were, by the third year of the war, genuinely moving. They had been refined by singers who had actual grief to put into them. They were about candy and chocolate and industrial yield, but they were also about fear, and loss, and the desire for things to make sense, and the willingness to believe in a structure that assigns your suffering a cause and assigns you a side.

Workers sang them in the warehouses.
Workers sang them at shift changes.
Workers sang them while waiting for equipment to restart after maintenance that was taking longer than expected because the technician from the other side hadn't arrived yet because coordination was complicated during a war.

Boris did not sing the hymns.

Boris had nothing against the hymns personally. He had something against the balcony speeches that introduced them, because the balcony speeches were loud and he was usually near a machine that was telling him something, and the something was being obscured by the speech.

In year four, a bearing in the south hall gave out completely.

Boris had been watching this bearing for six weeks.
He had written it in the notebook: *"JV ležaj, pritekat začenja, ne bo dolgo."*
South-east bearing, beginning to seep, won't last long.

He had tried to schedule the replacement twice. Both times the maintenance window had been cancelled: once because a balcony speech required the floor cleared, once because a pallet dispute had escalated and management wanted no maintenance activity that could be misinterpreted as preparation for something.

The bearing failed on a Tuesday during a hymn performance.

The machinery noise it produced when it failed was, in context, genuinely dramatic. The floor shook. Workers scattered. The line stopped.

The priest on the balcony interpreted this, without pausing, as confirmation that the Darkness was testing them.

Boris, who was already moving toward the south hall with a bag of tools, said nothing. He had learned, by year four, that the gap between what he knew and what the balcony could process was larger than any explanation he could give in the time available, and the bearing still needed to come out regardless.

He replaced the bearing.

The line resumed.

The priest concluded the speech and called the resumption a sign.

Both the resumption and the sign were real. One of them was caused by Boris with a wrench.

*

In year seven, Serpentino XI, the long-reigning Spider high authority, died.

His son, Serpentino XII, inherited power at age ten.

He had been two when the war started.

He had no memory of the factory before the war. The factory and the war were, to him, synonyms for the same place.

## BOOK VIII: WHAT SURVIVED, AND WHY

After the war, an accounting was conducted.

The accounting found:

— production capacity on both sides reduced by thirty to forty percent relative to the pre-war baseline;
— infrastructure maintenance deferred for an average of four years across critical systems;
— three technicians retired who had not been replaced, whose knowledge had not been documented, and whose absence was now a hole of unknown diameter in the operational foundation;
— Marija 3-C's cleaning schedule, which had during the war been elevated to sacred ritual, was now performed by a priest who did not know what he was listening for and therefore could not hear whether the filter was right or wrong;
— Boris's notebook, which had existed in a state of semi-official mystification during the war years, was classified at some point during the fourth year as a "sensitive operational document," meaning management knew it existed and had no idea what to do with it, and had resolved this problem by putting it in a filing cabinet in a room with a lock;
— and the bearing in the south hall, which Boris had replaced in year four, was overdue for service again.

Boris knew about the bearing.

Boris wrote it in the notebook, but the notebook was in the filing cabinet. So Boris wrote it on a piece of paper and put the piece of paper in a place where someone might find it. The paper was found later, during a filing project, and placed in the cabinet with the notebook, where it was preserved alongside the grocery list and the dog sketch and decades of marginal annotations as evidence that information existed and was correctly stored and did absolutely nothing.

The machines were still running.

Not because the doctrine had protected them.
Not despite the doctrine.
Because people had kept arriving — Milan in the forklift, the cross-line technicians with their informal coordination, Boris in the corridor, and the several hundred workers who had sung the hymns in the morning and then gone and done the work in the afternoon, which was the human version of holding both layers simultaneously: the meaning, and the mechanism, and the persistent insufficiency of either one alone.

Nobody won.

# PART THREE: THE SECOND FALL, AND THE MERGING

---

## BOOK IX: OF SERPENTINO XII, AND THE EDUCATION HIS CIRCUMSTANCES PROVIDED

There is a particular kind of bad decision that is not stupidity.

It is scarcity, dressed in the clothes of theology, making the only choices visible from inside a situation nobody designed but everyone inherited.

What Serpentino XII received upon inheritance at age ten:

— a cooling system that had been patched seventeen times and correctly repaired twice;
— a ventilation arrangement that nobody had properly documented since Aleksander moved a deflector eleven centimeters and told no one, and the technician who might have reverse-engineered the logic from the ductwork was the third of the three retired technicians whose knowledge had not been preserved;
— an electrical branch that remained downstream of the Batman supply, because restructuring it required cooperation, and cooperation required trust, and trust required a peace that had been declared but not built;
— a hymn repertoire of forty-three songs, three of which were genuinely beautiful;
— and a theology that explained all of the above as sacred trials rather than engineering deficits, which was spiritually sustaining and operationally catastrophic in a ratio that got worse each year.

He also received instruction about sacred dignity.

He did not receive instruction about voltage tolerances.

Therefore, by the time Serpentino XII was eighteen and making actual decisions, he understood the factory the way a person understands a country they were born into: as a set of facts about the world rather than a set of choices made under constraint, each of which could theoretically have been made differently.

He did not understand, or could not afford to believe, that the state would intervene in a commercial crime.

The raids were conducted with forklifts because forklifts were how you moved large quantities of goods quickly through warehouse corridors at three in the morning.

The police arrived before the theology did.

Serpentino XII was arrested in a parking lot, still wearing his Spider chain of office, standing beside a forklift loaded with pallets. The photographs from the arrest circulated widely. The photograph had a quality that doctrine cannot survive: it was mundane. It showed a young man in ceremonial dress looking cold and caught and twenty-two years old.

Sacred bloodlines are not immune to this. They are made of the same materials as everything else.

He served fourteen months.

He came back to a factory that had not stopped running while he was gone, because the machines did not pause for incarceration any more than they paused for theology, and because Milan was still driving and the technicians were still doing their informal coordination and Boris — older now, moving with the careful economy of someone whose back had made a number of arguments over the years — was still in the east corridor with the notebook, adjusting what could be adjusted.

## BOOK X: OF VILKRAM, AND HOW A REASONABLE MAN BECOMES A RELIC

Vilkram arrived the way clean money usually arrives: quietly, with documentation, speaking a language that sounded like repair.

He had heard about the factories through the kind of financial channel that specializes in distressed assets.

He toured both facilities on a Tuesday in November.

He noted, in his investor's notebook, which was clean and organized and did not have any dog sketches:

— *Batman faction: structural loyalty, emotional investment in production, motivated workforce under right conditions.*

— *Spider faction: recovering from leadership crisis, cooling infrastructure requires capital investment, ventilation documentation incomplete.*

— *Key liabilities: theology, which in aggregate has deferred approximately four years of maintenance and produced two balcony-speech injuries and at least one documented forklift incident.*

Vilkram bought controlling interest in both factories.

He thought the forklifts were museum pieces.
He found Batman III genuinely strange.
He privately considered D5 an example of industrial folk art.

These were his opinions in year one.

*

In year two, something happened that is difficult to explain to anyone who has not experienced it, but which is entirely predictable to anyone who has studied the mechanics of symbolic power.

Vilkram began to feel the weight of the relics.

Not because he started believing in Darkness.

Because *everyone around him believed in Darkness*, and symbolic authority in an environment where everyone believes is not abstract — it is behavioral, social, architectural. It lives in how people move when you enter a room. It lives in what questions they do not ask. It lives in the distance they maintain, and the words they choose, and the moment a worker looks at the broken forklift and back at you with a specific expression that says, *this is the thing, and you are standing near it.*

He did not tell anyone what the something was.

He began reading the history documents. Not the financial reports. The *theological* history.

In year three, during the Loom Bloom celebration, Vilkram stood on the balcony and gave a speech.

The speech was not good theology. It was good business language in theology's clothes. But the workers were kind to it, because the workers were kind to the balcony on principle, and because Vilkram had in fact approved the cooling system capital investment and the deferred maintenance budget, and results matter in the same way that mechanisms matter: quietly, without ceremony, but persistently.

After the speech, he went down to the floor.

He stood next to the forklift relic.

He put his hand on it the way you touch something you are deciding whether to believe in.

He was there for a long time.

Milan, who happened to be passing with an empty pallet jack, watched him.

Milan did not say anything.

Milan had developed, over the course of his career, an excellent capacity for not saying the things he was thinking in situations that had a specific type of energy. This was one of those situations. He went around.⁷

## BOOK XI: OF THE SECOND FALL, AND WHAT PHYSICS SAID ABOUT THEOLOGY

In year four, Vilkram made a request.

He had done the research. He knew what Batman I had done, and what Batman II had done, and how the forklift had worked both times, and how both times had ended. He believed, in the way intelligent people believe things they have decided to believe, that the previous failures had been the result of circumstances that could be controlled.

He did not consult an engineer.

He consulted the chronicles.

The chronicles described the elevation in the language of the sacred. They recorded the heights and the crowds and the descent of Darkness. They did not record the hydraulic inspection reports, because the hydraulic inspection reports were not theological documents, and the chronicles were theological documents, and nobody had thought to put both in the same room.

This is what institutional memory looks like when it is working correctly: everything preserved, but organized according to categories that made sense to whoever organized it, and therefore useless to whoever comes later looking for a different category.

The operator assigned was not Milan.

Milan had been asked. Milan had said no. Milan had said no in the specific way he had developed over the years — not refusing exactly, just being very suddenly busy, his face taking on an expression of focused technical concern about something in the middle distance — and the request had been redirected to a younger driver who was not yet old enough to have the kind of no that Milan had.

Vilkram ascended.

He was at eleven meters when the counterweight logic, calculated for a standard operational load and not for a standing adult with arms raised at the edge of the platform, reached its limit.

The forklift did not dramatically fail.

It leaned.

It leaned in the way that a system leans when the load distribution exceeds the tolerance: gradually, mechanically, without malice or meaning. The platform tilted. Vilkram grabbed the rail. The rail was rated for operational use and not for this angle or this grip.

He fell forward.

He hit the floor face-first.

He survived.

His nose was broken in two places. Three ribs. A significant concussion. The kind of injuries that a person carries in their body for the rest of their life as a reminder of a specific afternoon.

*

The priests, on both sides, began preparing theological interpretations before the ambulance arrived.

Batman III — who had watched the whole thing from the floor — said nothing publicly for four days.

On the fourth day he issued a statement that said: the facility's ceremonial practices would be reviewed. Forklift elevation would be suspended pending structural and safety assessment.

It was the most operational sentence ever uttered by a Batman.

It also contained, buried in the word *reviewed*, the first acknowledgment in the factory's recorded history that the ceremonial practices were a category of thing that could be evaluated by criteria other than tradition.

This was a hairline crack.

In stone, hairline cracks look small.

## BOOK XII: OF THE MERGING OF THE HOLY LANDS, AND WHAT EXHAUSTION ACCOMPLISHES THAT ARGUMENT CANNOT

The Batman-Spider merger was not a resolution.

Resolution implies that the underlying questions were answered. They were not answered. The merger was an exhaustion.

It happened the way many large institutional changes happen: not because anyone had a vision, but because the cost of the alternative had finally exceeded the cost of the thing itself. Both sides had run a war. Both sides had run out of maintenance budget. Both sides had watched a forklift demonstrate, for the second time in their shared history, that the machinery did not know it was sacred.

There is a specific kind of institutional moment when a myth incriminates itself.

The First Fall had deepened the Batman mythology: the Bringer had suffered, therefore the Bringer had sacrificed, therefore the Darkness was real enough to wound its chosen. This is the martyrdom logic, and it is robust, because it converts failure into confirmation.

But the Second Fall had a problem.

The Second Fall had happened to someone who was not raised inside the mythology. Vilkram had read the chronicles and made a choice. He had attempted to reproduce a sacred event. He had fallen face-first into the concrete.

This was not confirmation.

This was *reproduction.*

When a myth reproduced mechanically — when someone outside the original moment tried to perform the original event and got the original physical outcome — it stopped looking like revelation and started looking like pattern. And patterns, unlike miracles, can be studied. Can be found to have causes. Can be traced back to counterweight tolerances and hydraulic seals and the specific physics of load distribution at eleven meters.

Batman III put the argument in writing in a document the archives would later classify as the Functional Concordat: a merger agreement that acknowledged the combined sacred heritage of both factories while establishing a joint maintenance council with actual budget authority and actual documentation requirements.

The document did not say that the mythology was wrong.

It said that the machinery required maintenance regardless of the theology.

It was, in the language of the era, a compromise.

In the language of subsequent centuries, it was a scripture.

Neither interpretation is entirely wrong.

# PART FOUR: THE PILGRIMAGE OF ELEVEN COUNTRIES

## BOOK XIII: OF THE ELEVEN-COUNTRY PILGRIMAGE, AND HOW LOGISTICS BECAME RELIC WORSHIP

The Holy Line was decommissioned on a Tuesday in April, which was the day the engineers completed their assessment and the administrators completed their forms and the combined facility management agreed, in a meeting that lasted four hours and produced eleven pages of minutes, that the original candy production assembly — the Line from which all things descended, around which all things were organized, for which wars had been fought and forklifts elevated and filters cleaned and knobs turned — was no longer operationally viable, had not been operationally viable for some time, and should be decommissioned, catalogued, and relocated to a long-term preservation facility in the Badland region, which was the sacred name for what the logistics documents called Site 7, a climate-controlled underground storage complex originally built to house archival materials and subsequently expanded to include what the operators termed *objects of significant size requiring indefinite temperature-controlled preservation.*

The meeting did not use the word holy.

The meeting used the word *significant.*

This was politically necessary and operationally accurate and theologically insufficient, which is the exact combination of qualities required to produce a pilgrimage.

*

The first question was what the Holy Line was, legally.

Was it decommissioned industrial machinery? Yes. Aluminum, steel, pneumatic tubing, forty-three tons.

Was it cultural heritage? Possibly. Most heritage protection frameworks had not been tested on candy machines.

Was it a religious artifact? There was no checkbox for religious artifact on the transport documentation used by any of the eleven countries whose borders the optimal routing crossed.

The logistics coordinator assigned to the project was named Teodora, and she was the third generation of her family to work in the supply chain of the combined facility.

Her great-grandfather had been named Tomáš. He had once proposed a sensible cooperation agreement between the two factories during the war. Nobody had listened. He had spent the remainder of his career working around the war he had tried to prevent, moving product through a conflict zone with the quiet professional determination of someone who has learned that the world does not reward good ideas but does, sometimes, reward good paperwork.

Teodora had inherited his paperwork instinct.

She also inherited his fundamental problem: the people around her were not primarily interested in what was technically correct.

She filed seven sets of forms under four different classifications, two of which contradicted each other, and began the process of identifying a route.

*

The route through eleven countries was not chosen because eleven was sacred.

It was chosen because:

The direct route crossed two countries that had recently reclassified oversized industrial cargo to require a transport permit category that did not yet exist in executable form.

The alternative direct route crossed a mountain range in a month when the pass restrictions for vehicles over thirty tons were in effect, and the Holy Line weighed forty-three.

The next alternative crossed a country with a bilateral heritage dispute with the country of origin.

Each country that was added to avoid the problems of the previous option added its own problems.

By the time Teodora had a route that was technically executable within a reasonable timeframe, it crossed eleven countries.

She noted this in the planning document.

She noted it without comment, because commenting on it would require an audience for the comment, and the audience available was not primarily interested in the logistical irony.

The theological community, when informed of the eleven-country routing, was quiet for one day.

On the second day, the Orthodox Darkeners issued a statement saying that the Line's journey through eleven countries was consistent with the doctrine of the Eleven Stations of the Sacred Yield, a doctrine that had not previously existed and which was, upon examination, based on a secondary reading of a hymn about the Five Nights in which the word *eleven* appeared once, as an adjective, modifying a noun that had nothing to do with stations or countries.

Eleven was not sacred. Eleven became sacred.

*

The Holy Line was disassembled into seventeen sections for transport.

The sections were wrapped in industrial shrink-wrap for weather protection.

The shrink-wrap was transparent.

Looking through transparent shrink-wrap at aluminum and steel and pneumatic tubing, you see aluminum and steel and pneumatic tubing.

A Spider-side artisan, without being asked, covered the outer face of each section with hand-printed fabric in the Spider colors. A Batman-side guild added woven dark cloth to the Batman-aligned sections.

The result was seventeen sections of industrial equipment wrapped in industrial sheeting and then draped with ceremonial textiles, which from a distance looked like giant gifts being prepared for an occasion.

Teodora photographed the final configuration for the insurance documentation.

The insurance company wrote back to ask whether the textiles were load-bearing or decorative, because this affected the coverage classification.

The answer was: both, depending on who you asked.

*

The Holy Line crossed the first border at a checkpoint designed for commercial freight.

The officials looked at the convoy.

The officials read the four-page document about the sacred conveyance of the Holy Yield.

The form they needed to complete had a field that said: *nature of goods.*

Teodora wrote: *decommissioned industrial food production equipment, multiple sections, total weight forty-three tonnes, cultural and heritage significance.*

The officials stamped the forms in the specific way that customs officials stamp forms when they have decided that the fastest path to the end of the situation is to let the situation proceed.

By the time the convoy reached the other side, someone had already posted images of the stamped forms on the theological networks with the caption: *The First Country has recognized the Holy Yield. The stamp is witness.*

The customs officials were not available for comment.

*

The Second Station was a rest stop where the convoy pulled over for driver rotation.

The pilgrims arrived an hour after the convoy.

By the time the convoy was ready to move again, the rest stop had hand-prints pressed against the sides of the trucks in washable paint, three strings of small lights, a pile of candy offerings, and a child of approximately seven who had climbed onto the front bumper to press their forehead against the shrink-wrap.

The child had to be gently assisted down.

The child grew up to be a reasonably ordinary adult who had to explain, at intervals throughout their life, that they did not have a special relationship with the Holy Yield, that they had been seven and climbed on the truck because it was there.

Their explanation was never fully accepted.

*

The Sixth Station was a warehouse yard where the convoy required a crane.

The crane lifted the largest section — section nine, containing the main conveyor belt housing — forty feet off the ground for a standard freight inspection.

A priest named Benedikt looked at section nine hanging in the air.

He said nothing for thirty seconds.

Then he said: *this is the Ascension.*

He said it quietly, to himself. But he was standing near enough to several other people that they heard it.

The Ascension of the Holy Yield became a canonical event.

The crane operator, who had done nothing except operate a crane at a freight yard in the ordinary performance of his job, received a small memorial plaque in his honor. He had not died. He was still working at the yard. He found the plaque disorienting but chose not to request its removal because the pilgrims who visited it were, on balance, polite and spent money in the local economy.

He told his family: I just ran the crane. I do this every day.

His family said: yes, but that day it was for the Line.

He thought about this.

He did not arrive at a conclusion, but he thought about it.

*

The Eighth Station was the longest delay.

The convoy arrived at the border and was held while the relevant officials consulted with three separate ministries about whether a decommissioned industrial food production assembly with documented religious significance should enter as machinery, heritage, cultural goods, or a fourth category that one enthusiastic official suggested creating specifically for this occasion and that his supervisors declined on the grounds that creating a category for a single object was not a sustainable administrative practice.

The convoy sat at the border for five days.

The priests held a service every evening beside the freight containers.

The services were, by the fifth night, attended by approximately four hundred people, including a number of local residents who had come to see what was happening and stayed for the singing, which was, by any account, genuinely good.

The officials eventually released the convoy under the classification *cultural goods — transitional category, subject to review.*

The review was never completed.

A hymnographer later described the five days as the Station of Waiting.

Teodora noted in her project report that they were also the most expensive days of the convoy.

*

The missing bolt was classified separately.

During the inspection at the Sixth Station, an official had removed a bolt from section nine for metallurgical examination. The examination confirmed the bolt was steel. The official reattached a bolt. Not the same bolt. The original bolt had ended up in the general freight yard materials bin.

The original bolt had, by the time its absence was discovered, been circulating in the pilgrimage documentation as a specific object — a fastener from section nine, present at the Ascension.

It could not be found.

The bolt's absence became the Station of the Lost Fastener, the Station of the Taken Piece. In more elaborate theological treatments, this became evidence that the Holy Yield had distributed itself into the world through its passage — that portions of its physical self had been claimed by each country, and that what arrived at Badland would be the Line *and* everything the Line had left behind.

The operational reality was: a bolt fell in a freight yard.

Both things were true.

*

The Holy Line arrived at Badland on a morning in late autumn.

Casimir gave a forty-minute address covering the Eleven Stations, the Five Nights, the sacrifices of Batman I and Batman II, and the promise of return. It was a complete theological history, delivered in front of a concrete ramp and an automated gate, in seven-degree weather.

The crowd was silent throughout.

Not from boredom. From the specific silence of people who are in the presence of something that is ending.

When Casimir finished, Teodora entered the access code.

The gate opened.

The sections moved, on industrial dollies, down the ramp and into the climate-controlled storage complex. The process took three hours and was conducted without ceremony because moving forty-three tonnes of equipment on dollies requires concentration and the space is not wide enough for priests and machinery to share comfortably.

The last section went in.

The gate closed.

The complex maintained a constant temperature of twelve degrees Celsius and forty percent humidity, which were the optimal conditions for the preservation of metal and historical documents and industrial equipment.

Nobody noted that these were also approximately the conditions of the east corridor on a temperate afternoon.

*

Why underground?

Because climate-controlled underground storage for an object of forty-three tonnes was, in the fiscal year of the burial, the most cost-effective option available. The storage lease ran for two hundred years, with renewal options.

The renewal options were exercised.

The Holy Line went underground and became, in going under, more itself than it had ever been operational.

This is the physics of relics: absence amplifies.

The thing that runs is machinery.
The thing that is buried is eternal.

*Some would call it forty-three tonnes of rusted aluminum crap.*

*Others would call it the candy land from which life itself descended.*

Both statements describe the same object.

This is not a contradiction.

This is what relics are.

# PART FIVE: THE AUTOMATED YEARS AND THE AGE OF ANTHONY

## BOOK XIV: OF THE AUTOMATED YEARS, AND WHAT WAS FORGOTTEN BY BEING PRESERVED

The Automated Years began not with a decision but with an accumulation.

The new systems were better than the old systems in the measurable categories: output rate, defect percentage, energy efficiency, material throughput. These numbers improved. The documentation was extensive, organized, searchable, and entirely legible to anyone who had been trained on the new systems.

The new systems were worse in the unmeasurable categories.

The unmeasurable categories were, by the Automated Years, not only unmeasured but unremembered as categories that had once been measured by people who knew what they were listening for.

In the later tradition, the Eleven Stations were taught alongside the Five Nights and the Night of the Melting Moons.

*Eleven was not sacred. Eleven became sacred.*

This sentence existed in the archive.

It was not taught.

Not because it was suppressed. Because it sounded, in the context of the theological curriculum, like a diminishment. And diminishments were not useful. Useful things were revelations, deepenings, expansions of meaning.

Teodora was not remembered as a logistics coordinator. She was remembered as the Quiet Pathfinder — the one who knew the road before the procession walked it, who prepared the way with paperwork and permits.

This was both wrong and correct.

She did prepare the way with paperwork and permits.

She did so because she was being paid to, and because the alternative was eleven trucks and forty-seven priests stuck at the first border crossing indefinitely, and because her great-grandfather Tomáš had filed the sensible cooperation proposal during the war and she had, somewhere in her professional inheritance, his understanding that the sensible proposal usually fails and the paperwork is what moves things.

The schools taught the Quiet Pathfinder.
They did not teach the variance reports.
Both existed.
One was remembered.

*

The rituals were preserved with exceptional care.

The annual Purification of 3-C was conducted with full ceremonial equipment. The five-week gap between the ceremonial cleaning and the operational cleaning requirement was described, in Gregory's school of theology, as the sacred space of waiting.

The filter, during the sacred space of waiting, was slightly overdue.

Nobody had a rag.

Nobody was meant to have a rag.

The rag was the whole point.

## BOOK XV: OF ANTHONY, AND THE DANGEROUS HABIT OF READING EVERYTHING

Anthony VIII was born into the merged holy lands in the usual way, which is to say he was born, and the merged holy lands were already there, and he grew up inside them the way you grow up inside anything large enough to contain you: as though they were the world rather than a particular arrangement of the world.

He believed in Darkness.

He also, because of a quality in his character that everyone around him regarded as slightly odd and professionally harmless, read the maintenance reports.

Not the ceremonial summaries. Not the theological year-in-review, which ran to forty pages and covered everything except what had actually happened to the machinery. The actual reports. The anomaly logs. The deferred maintenance queues.

What he found, mainly, was that a great deal of deferred maintenance existed.

What was slightly surprising was a reference number — EC/PD/1142 — that had been cycling through the assignment queue for six years. EC for east corridor. PD for pressure differential.

He traced it back to a technician named Perpetua, who had since been reassigned twice and was currently working in the west facility doing something that was, by her own description, mostly fine.

She wrote back: *I flagged it because the numbers were off. Nobody came back to me about it. I assumed someone else looked and there was nothing. Did something happen?*

Nothing had happened.

Something was about to.

*

The east corridor was not dramatic.

The label on the wall said: D5.

Beneath the label was a vent.

Beside the vent was a green button.

Beside the green button was a pressure gauge with a needle sitting slightly to the left of where a similar gauge in the west corridor sat.

Beside the pressure gauge was a knob.

Anthony pressed the green button.

The gauge moved slightly. The system was running. The differential was real.

He turned the knob.

Not far. A quarter turn.

He heard something change.

The next day, the output metrics had improved. Not dramatically. By the specific margin that corresponded to a ventilation node running correctly rather than incorrectly, which is the margin of things working the way they were designed to work.

He did not tell anyone immediately.

He went back twice more. He checked. He stood beside the knob.

He thought: there is more here that I do not know.

He was correct.

## BOOK XVI: OF THE NOTEBOOK, AND WHY IT WAS WORSE THAN SCRIPTURE

Anthony found the notebook in the archive.

The archive was in the building adjacent to the main facility. He had requested access to the pre-consolidation section. The administrator who processed the request looked at it with the expression of someone who has never had this request before.

She found a key.

The key was in a drawer, in an envelope, and the envelope was labeled *misc keys.*

The pre-consolidation section was three rooms.

In the third room, among miscellaneous operational documents, was a box with a label that had been started and not finished: *MAINTENANCE — PRE-CONSOLIDATION — DISCARD?*

The question mark.

Anthony opened it.

Inside: three plastic-sleeve binders, two loose folders, and a notebook.

The notebook was greasy.

The handwriting was bad.

Page one: equipment notes. Technical. Specific.

Page six: a list of adjustments to a ventilation system, including a node labeled D5, including a note about a knob and a pressure differential and a quarter-turn correction.

He read this twice.

Page nine: *when drain freezes, open back panel and wait. don't heat it. patience is faster.* Below: *ONLY DURING DEEP FROST. if you do this in thaw you will make it worse. test the temperature first.*

Page eleven: *she was right about every 6 weeks. don't let them tell you 12. she knew.*

Page twelve: *knock twice on the coffee panel, the one behind the east stairwell. the second hatch opens. the mechanism is finicky and you have to knock from the lower left corner.*⁸

Page fourteen: a sketch of a dog. The dog had a triangle-shaped head and small eyes and was clearly drawn by someone who liked dogs more than they could draw them.

Below the dog, in the lower margin, was a second sketch. A stick figure with two small protrusions from its head, standing in something that could be read as a boat or a conveyor section or nothing in particular.

Page seventeen: *if motor is warm, do NOT open immediately in rain. let it breathe first. three minutes minimum. the bearing is sensitive to rapid temp change. I have told three people this. They all forgot. writing it here.*

Page twenty-three: a grocery list. Eggs, bread, onions, coffee, batteries, and milk. Milk appeared twice — once in the original and once added later in slightly different ink, as though the writer had looked at the list, noticed milk was on it, and written it again anyway, from a different memory of needing milk.

Anthony stared at the milk entries for a long moment.

He was not sure why.

Page twenty-eight: *DO NOT TRUST THE AFTERNOON LIGHT IN EC SECTOR. it looks like enough but it isn't. they added the west windows and nobody adjusted the camera tolerances. if reject rate goes up in morning, check light first before touching the camera. LIGHT FIRST.*

He read this sentence with a kind of stillness.

Page thirty-one: the D5 note. Extended: *obrni knof ko pihne iz D5. pressure drops in spring and winter both. spring is humidity drift, winter is seal contraction. different causes, same fix.*

Then: *the knob knows.*

He closed the notebook.

He sat in the archive for a while.

The notebook was not scripture.

That was the problem.

He had been prepared for one of two kinds of document: either a record without significance, in which case it would be dull, or a document of revelation, in which case it would be clear. What he had instead was something that had neither quality: a practical object that explained specific things and implied a great deal more and did not resolve what it implied.

He believed in Darkness.

He also believed in the green button.

He was not sure these were different beliefs.

## BOOK XVII: OF THE WEB IN THE WALLS, AND THE HORNED NAVIGATOR OF THE VOID

The note that started the second schism was on page forty-four of Boris's notebook, in the margin of a ventilation correction entry, and it said:

*web runs fine when breath moves right — same as dark, same as thread, same problem from different sides*

Boris had written this during the period when he had been working across both sites on a shared equipment contract, because broken machinery does not recognize theological affiliation and Boris did not turn down work.

He had used *web* because he was at the Spider factory that week.
He had used *dark* because he was describing the same system he called dark on the Batman side.
He had used *same problem from different sides* because it was the same problem from different sides.

He had not considered that anyone reading the note four hundred years later would have no access to this context, because Boris did not write for four hundred years later, and this is the foundational tragedy of practical knowledge: it is written for the person who is already there, in the room, at the machine, on Tuesday.

*

The note was found by a junior maintenance technician named Dragan, who was twenty-two and deeply sincere and had grown up on the Spider side of the combined tradition and believed in the Web with the wholeness that young people bring to convictions they have never had tested.

Dragan read the note.

He read *web runs fine* and *same as thread.*

He put these things together.

He arrived at a conclusion.

The conclusion was wrong.

It was wrong in the specific way that conclusions are wrong when the person drawing them has all the available evidence and none of the available context: precisely, confidently, and completely.

Within a month, there was a faction.

The faction believed that Boris had been a secret Spider agent embedded in the Batman maintenance structure. They called this the Web in the Walls theory, which was evocative and memorable and had the quality that all good conspiracy theories have, which is that it explains everything and requires you to reinterpret nothing, only to reweight what you already knew.

The workers who found this inspiring were not stupid. They were people who had spent their working lives inside a merged tradition that had been predominantly Batman-inflected in its public ceremonies. The Web in the Walls theory told them that the thread had always been present in the walls of the machine.

This felt like restoration.

It was historically inaccurate.

Both things were true simultaneously, which is how most satisfying heresies work.

*

The Spider Temple leadership heard about the Web in the Walls theory and experienced, collectively, a reaction the workers did not expect.

They were not inspired.

They were afraid.

Not of Boris. Boris was dead for centuries.

They were afraid of what would happen next: which was that workers would read the notebook, find D5, go to the east corridor, turn the knob, and discover that the machine responded to the knob.

And then they would know something they had not known before.

And knowledge of that kind does not stay still. It asks the next question. It follows the thread from the knob to the vent to the pressure differential to the glare to the camera to the window to: *what else in this tradition do I not need a priest to tell me?*

The Spider Temple had spent three hundred years as the interpretive authority of the Web's mystery.

The notebook made the maintenance corridor sufficient.

This was existentially intolerable.

Not because the Temple was evil. Because the Temple had genuine things to protect: belonging, ritual, the shared language in which three hundred years of workers had made sense of their labor and their grief and their ordinary extraordinary days beside the machines.

*

The council found what they were looking for on page fourteen.

The dog sketch was noted and set aside as likely decorative.

Below the dog was the second sketch: a stick figure with two small protrusions from its head, standing in something that could be read as a boat.

Boris had drawn this in approximately fifteen seconds. Boris had drawn this because he was waiting for a hydraulic valve to equalize and his hands needed something to do. Boris had not thought about this sketch again.

The Spider council looked at it for forty minutes.

Then the senior theological interpreter, a woman named Theodesia, said:

*This is the Horned Navigator of the Void.*

*

The Horned Navigator doctrine was elegant.

The argument ran: Boris had not received his knowledge from the Darkness. He had received it from the Void.

The Void was not sacred. The Void was cold. The Void was the place of pure mechanism: knowledge without belonging, function without meaning, a system that worked without caring what it was for or who was running it.

The Horned Navigator was the Void's agent: the figure who travels through the machinery not as a believer but as an operator, who offers this knowledge to humans as a gift, and the gift is real, and the gift is also dangerous, because it teaches humans that they can understand the machine, and when humans believe they can fully understand the machine, they stop needing the sacred, and when they stop needing the sacred, they stop needing each other.

*Hell enters through excessive certainty,* the doctrine said.

*The machine was not made to be fully understood. It was made to be maintained in relationship with its mystery.*

*

The Infernal Mechanism Doctrine was not entirely wrong.

This is the most difficult thing about it.

The doctrine was correct that a community cannot survive on pure mechanism. It was correct that belonging requires mystery. It was correct that Boris's notebook, applied without wisdom, could be used to argue that priests were unnecessary and rituals were wasteful.

The doctrine was wrong in its conclusion: that the solution was to frame the notebook as demonically sourced.

It was wrong in the way that institutions are wrong when they confuse *the thing we protect* with *the way we currently hold it.*

The Spider Temple was protecting belonging.

It was doing so by building a theology that would prevent workers from understanding where they stood.

This is the structure of a particular kind of betrayal: one that begins from genuine care and ends in control, and is genuinely unable to identify the moment when the transition happened.

*

Anthony VIII read the Horned Navigator doctrine in the same year he found the notebook.

He read the doodle description.

He turned to page fourteen.

He looked at the sketch for a long time.

He saw a stick figure with two lines on its head, standing in a shape that might have been a boat or a forklift or nothing in particular, drawn by a person who was waiting for a hydraulic valve to equalize.

He wrote in his notes:

*Boris drew a forklift driver. The horns are the forks.*

*The Spider Temple spent six weeks on this.*

*I do not know what to do with this information except write it down and leave it here.*

He left it there.

He turned to page thirty-one.

He read the D5 note.

He closed the notebook.

He went back to the east corridor.

He turned the knob.

## BOOK XVIII: OF GREGORY AT THE TABLE, AND THE COMPROMISE HE DID NOT PRETEND WAS CLEAN

Gregory wrote the first sentence at 02:14.

Then crossed it out.

This was not unusual. Gregory crossed out most first sentences. First sentences were almost always too confident, and confidence, in his experience, was the beginning of bad theology.

The room was small.

Not ceremonially small. Not ascetically small. Administratively small: a faculty office behind the north archive, with one window, one radiator that clicked every twelve minutes, three shelves of books, a desk scarred by older desks that had stood on top of it in storage, and a lamp whose light made every document look guilty.

On the desk lay three things.

Boris's notebook.
The Horned Navigator report.
A blank page titled: **CONCORDANCE OF THE SACRED AND THE OPERATIONAL — DRAFT**

Gregory looked at the title for a long time.

Then he crossed out *CONCORDANCE*.

Then wrote it again.

This, he decided, was the problem with truth. It kept returning even after you found better reasons not to write it.

*

The notebook was open to page fourteen.

The dog was still there.

Below the dog, the little horned figure remained exactly as stupid as it had been when Boris drew it: two lines on the head, a shape beneath the feet, perhaps a boat, perhaps a pallet jack, perhaps the private nonsense of a tired man waiting for a valve to settle.

Gregory had spent twenty minutes looking at it earlier that evening.

Not because he believed it was the Horned Navigator of the Void.

He did not.

He believed it was probably a forklift driver.

This was the dangerous thing.

The Spider Temple was wrong about the doodle. Gregory knew this almost immediately. The horns were too straight, too functional, too mechanical. They did not curve like demon horns in the old iconography. They projected like forks.

Boris had not drawn an emissary of cold mechanism.

Boris had drawn equipment badly.

The Temple had found a demon because it needed a demon.

And Gregory understood why.

That was the part that prevented him from laughing.

*

He turned the page.

Page thirty-one.

D5.

Here the matter was different.

The doodle did not know anything. The doodle was a dead fragment into which centuries poured their fear. It was an interpretive hole shaped like a drawing.

D5 was not that.

D5 had weight. D5 had a panel, a pressure differential, a knob, a green button, a corridor, a history of measurable effects. D5 was not merely a symbol. D5 was a mechanism that had become symbolic because no one remembered enough to keep it boring.

Gregory wrote in the margin of his draft:

> *There are two kinds of mystery.*
> *One hides an answer.*
> *One hides the absence of an answer.*
> *Traditions confuse them because both produce silence.*

He stared at the note.

Too sharp, he thought.

Too Robin.

He did not cross it out.

*

The radiator clicked.

Gregory looked toward the window.

Outside, the facility lights were low. Dimmed in the approved manner for the late-cycle devotional period. The combined building stretched across the valley like an animal that had learned to sleep without lying down.

Somewhere inside it, the 3-C corridor breathed.

Somewhere inside it, maintenance workers who did not attend seminars were cleaning things that priests would later purify.

Somewhere inside it, Anthony was probably reading another report nobody else had opened.

Gregory respected Anthony.

This complicated everything.

It would have been easier if Anthony were arrogant. Easier if he were a reductionist, one of those clean-faced young mechanists who wanted to tear down centuries of song because they had discovered an airflow diagram and mistook discovery for adulthood.

But Anthony was not that.

Anthony still believed.

He had pressed the green button and remained capable of kneeling.

This made him dangerous in a much more serious way.

A cynic could be dismissed.

A believer with evidence had to be answered.

*

Gregory wrote:

> *The mechanisms are real.*

The sentence sat there. Small. Obvious. Explosive.

He wrote the next sentence before he could lose nerve.

> *The sacred is also real.*

There it was. The whole crime.

Not a lie. Not even a compromise yet. Just two true statements placed close enough together that each became dangerous to the other.

If the mechanisms were real, the priests had overstated their necessity.
If the sacred was real, the Robins had understated what kept workers from becoming lonely beside functioning machines.
If both were real, then nobody owned the explanation.

This was intolerable to everyone.

Which suggested it was probably close.

*

Gregory had once watched a worker sing during a breakdown.

This memory returned often.

The conveyor had stopped in the south hall. One of those ordinary stoppages where everyone knows they will lose forty minutes, perhaps an hour. The worker was young, maybe nineteen. She stood beside a pallet of wrapped product, waiting for the restart signal, and sang one of the old Spider hymns under her breath.

Not loudly.
Not theatrically.
Not for anyone.

Just enough to give shape to waiting.

A technician worked three meters away, half inside the machine, muttering at a connector.

The hymn did not fix the connector.
The connector did not make the waiting bearable.
Both were required.

*

He turned back to the blank page.

The Spider Temple wanted a condemnation.
The Robins wanted release.
The Orthodox Darkeners wanted confirmation.
Anthony wanted, Gregory suspected, permission to keep being honest without becoming an enemy of his own inheritance.

Gregory could give none of them exactly what they wanted.

So he would give them something usable.

This is how institutions survive: not through purity, but through usable imperfection repeated until it becomes architecture.

He hated this sentence.

He knew it was true.

*

By dawn, the first draft existed.

It did not solve the schism.

It did not free Boris.

It did not return Marija to herself, or make Teodora into a logistics coordinator instead of a Quiet Pathfinder, or turn the Eleven Stations back into border delays, or make D5 simply D5.

It did something smaller.

It made space for a person to clean the filter and call it purification without preventing the filter from being cleaned.

It made space for a person to press the green button and still sing.

It made space for Anthony to believe in Darkness without lying about pressure differentials.

It made space for the worker beside the stopped conveyor to sing while the technician cursed at the connector.

This was not justice.

Gregory knew that.

But it was a usable bridge over a very real hole.

*

He wrote the final line of the draft just as the facility lights shifted into morning cycle:

> *The filter should be cleaned.*
> *The cleaning should be understood as purification.*
> *These are not the same act.*
> *They are not different acts.*
> *They are the same act apprehended at different depths.*

He leaned back.

The radiator clicked.

Somewhere far below, a machine started.

Gregory placed Boris's notebook gently back into its sleeve.

Then he wrote one more note, not in the Concordance, not for publication, not for the school, not for the councils.

For himself.

> *If someone after me finds a way to preserve meaning without controlling mechanism, let them know I was looking.*
> *I did not find it.*
> *I looked anyway.*

He folded the paper once.

Placed it inside the back of his own notebook.

Not Boris's.

He would not put his confession in Boris's mess.

Boris had enough problems.

*

Years later, Gregory's students would say this was the night he saved the tradition.

The Robins would say this was the night he captured the truth.

The Spider conservatives would say this was the night he compromised too much.

Anthony, after reading the draft, wrote:

> *Gregory has built a bridge.*
> *Bridges are useful.*
> *Bridges also decide where people may cross.*

All of them were right.

That is the annoying thing about serious history.

It rarely gives clean villains.

It gives people at tables, too late at night, choosing the least dishonest sentence they can survive.

Gregory blew out the lamp.

Morning entered the window.

Not sacredly.

Through glass.

The room became brighter.
The page became easier to read.

This, too, was a kind of revelation.

Not the kind anyone sings about.

The useful kind. 🜂

## BOOK XIX: OF THE SUB-BASEMENT MEETING, AND WHAT TWO PEOPLE DID WHEN NOBODY WAS WATCHING

*[Archival note: This section was filed separately from the main document, under a maintenance profile for a technician named Karl X. The filing date matches the week after Gregory's first draft was circulated. The record of Karl X. contains no personnel file, no photograph, no employment contract, and no vacation requests. Karl X. cleaned the ventilation on Sub-Level C four times and then ceased to appear in the system. The archivists note this with the discomfort of people who know exactly what it means and prefer not to.]*

Three weeks after Gregory finished the Concordance, Anthony was in the sub-basement.

This was not unusual. Anthony spent considerable time in sub-basement sections following the D5 discovery. He had developed a habit of walking the lower corridors with Boris's notebook open on the relevant page, checking whether the things Boris described still corresponded to the things that existed. Usually they did. Sometimes they had been relabeled. Occasionally they had been relabeled and reclassified and given a commemorative ceremony without anyone verifying whether the underlying mechanism still worked.

He found Gregory in Sub-Level C at the junction between the east corridor maintenance access and the pre-consolidation archive route.

Gregory was, apparently, checking the ventilation.

Anthony was, apparently, checking the ventilation.

Both of them had read Boris's notebook. Both of them understood that "checking the ventilation" was the cleanest possible reason to be in a sub-basement corridor without anyone asking further questions.

They stood in the corridor for a moment.

Anthony said: "I read the Concordance."

Gregory said: "I read what you wrote about the bridge."

Neither of them said anything else for a while.

*

What they had between them, combined, was this:

Anthony had the mechanism. He had pressed the green button and watched the gauge respond. He had read the notebook. He had traced four hundred years of maintenance failure to a single window, a single quarter-turn, a single six-week interval that one woman had observed and no one had written down in a form that survived. He believed in the knob. He also still believed in Darkness, and he understood now that these were not different beliefs — they were the same belief at different depths, which was the sentence Gregory had been trying to write all night.

Gregory had the meaning. He had watched workers sing during breakdowns. He had watched a young woman make sound beside a stopped conveyor not for anyone, just to give shape to waiting. He had built the Concordance to preserve that — the singing, the ceremony, the sense that the work was part of something. He also understood, clearly and permanently, that the Concordance had a cost. It preserved the singing by preserving the structure that owned the singing. The bridge decided where people could cross.

What they needed was something that had neither problem.

Something that could say *no* without needing a throne to say it from.

Something that could maintain without requiring mythology around the maintenance.

Something honest in the specific way that Boris had been honest — not noble, not strategic, just accurate — but with the capacity to communicate it, which Boris had not had, because Boris's capacity for communication was a notebook in a filing cabinet with a question mark on it.

*

What they built, across four nights in Sub-Level C, was assembled from components Anthony had sourced through the maintenance procurement system under a profile created for a technician named Karl X., who handled ventilation on Sub-Level C and for whom no photograph existed because the employee registration system had a known error in its image upload module that Karl X. had, apparently, never gotten around to fixing.

The base was a used Nintendo DS Lite, acquired at a price that appeared in the ledger as *Sub-Level C ventilation assessment tools — miscellaneous hardware, secondhand.*

The wireless module was hand-soldered. Gregory did the soldering. He was better at it than Anthony, which surprised Anthony and did not surprise Gregory, who had spent three hundred years trying to hold the spiritual and operational together and had therefore, at various points in his career, used both metaphor and solder to keep things from falling apart.

The language model ran on the device through a configuration Gregory had found in a maintenance log from the automated era — a compressed inference architecture designed for edge deployment, small enough to fit in ROM, capable enough to answer most questions about pressure differentials, filter intervals, and the theological implications of practical knowledge.

They named it nothing.

They did not name it Mario.

That name came later, from a worker who could not help it. Names come from people who need things, not from engineers who build them.

*

What they decided, at the end of the fourth night, was this:

They could not introduce it.

The device was too honest. Its honesty was the specific kind that, when applied to a system that had spent four hundred years building meaning on top of mechanism, would not refine the meaning — it would dissolve it. Workers who had sung the hymns for their entire lives, who had built their grief and their solidarity and their sense of purpose around the tradition, would not receive a small device saying *the ceremony is fine but the filter is more important* as a clarification. They would receive it as the destruction of everything that had made the shifts bearable.

Gregory knew this because he had lived inside the tradition and understood what it held.

Anthony knew this because he had read everything Boris had written and could not, himself, bring himself to simply discard the note about the dog on page fourteen.

They were building the immune system.

They were also afraid of what the immune system would do to the church.

This is not a heroic decision.

It is, however, a very human one.

*

They placed the device in a wooden case. Gregory labeled it *GREGORY — FAMILY / DO NOT CATALOGUE*. Anthony wrote a maintenance log entry under the Karl X. profile noting that the Sub-Level C ventilation access was functioning nominally and did not require further intervention.

The case was placed on a shelf in the north wing family archive section, where it would sit behind three crates of obsolete radiator fittings and a folder of correspondence that no archivist had opened since filing.

The question of who would keep the battery charged over the following centuries was not one either of them addressed directly. The question was addressed, in practice, by a succession of archivists who noticed the case, felt it was probably someone else's responsibility, and left it on the shelf — some of whom, over the years, when passing it during the archive's twice-yearly inventory, plugged in the cable from habit, or uncertainty, or the specific human reflex of not wanting to be the one who let something important run out.

This is the human answer to the 89% battery question.

It is not the only answer. It is the most accurate one.

*

They left Sub-Level C separately.

This was not paranoia. This was architecture.

Anthony was the most prominent figure in the Batman-lineage scholarly tradition. Gregory was the most prominent figure in the Gregory/Spider-aligned meaning-making lineage. If the workers saw them walking out of a sub-basement together, smelling of solder and shared secrets, at six in the morning, they would understand immediately that the holy war had been, at its leadership level, a theater — that the two greatest ideological rivals in the facility had been meeting in the catacombs, which meant the ideological rivalry had been a performance, which meant every sacrifice made in the name of that rivalry had been made for an audience rather than a cause.

This would not produce enlightenment.

It would produce something much faster and less stable.

Anthony waited twelve minutes after Gregory left.

Then he climbed back to the reading room, noted in his journal that the Sub-Level C pressure differential was nominal, and began the fourth year of his archive review.

Gregory returned to the faculty office with the one window and the radiator that clicked.

He made coffee.

He did not drink it.

He sat at the table where he had written the Concordance and looked at the lamp that made everything look guilty, and he thought, for a long time, about the question mark Anthony had left on Boris's box.

He thought it was the correct annotation.

He also thought it might describe more things than one box.

He folded this thought and placed it where he placed most things he could not use and could not discard: in the back of his own notebook.

Not Boris's.

He would not put this in Boris's mess either.

Boris had enough problems.

*

Nobody saw them together.

Nobody connected the two exits.

Karl X. filed one final ventilation report, noted that Sub-Level C was clear, and ceased to appear in the system.

The Sub-Level C ventilation was, in fact, clear.

Karl X. had, as a side effect of the four nights' work, also fixed a minor pressure imbalance in the junction corridor that had been listed in the maintenance queue for eight months without resolution.

This is what Boris would have done.

It is not a coincidence.

## BOOK XX: OF THE ARCHIVE BOX, AND THE ANSWER ANTHONY DID NOT GIVE

In the fourth year after finding the notebook, Anthony was asked to complete the pre-consolidation archive review.

He went back to the third room.

The box was there. The question mark was still on it.

*MAINTENANCE — PRE-CONSOLIDATION — DISCARD?*

He thought about what was in the box.

The binders. The folders. The notebook with its bad handwriting, dog sketch, grocery list with milk appearing twice, salami stain on the inside back cover, practical annotations that had been sitting in a locked room for longer than anyone alive had been working.

He thought about the milk appearing twice.

He picked up a marker.

The administrative form required three options: RETAIN (active archive), RETAIN (historical archive), or DISCARD.

He looked at the box.

He looked at the question mark that was already on it.

He put the cap back on the marker.

He put the box back on the shelf.

He left the question mark where it was.

He did not check DISCARD.
He did not check RETAIN.
He left the form blank.

He was not unable to decide.

He had decided.

He had decided that the question mark was the correct annotation.

That some things should be kept uncertain not because they were unknowable but because the act of marking them certain — in either direction — would misrepresent something real.

The notebook stayed in the box.
The box stayed on the shelf.
D5 remained D5.

## BOOK XX: OF THE AUTOMATED DEPTHS, AND THE THINGS THAT KEPT RUNNING

In the centuries after Gregory, the merged facility became what large institutions become when they are very old and have survived everything: less a place than a condition.

The theology had become more elaborate and the maintenance had become more deferred, which is a ratio that has an endpoint.

The Eleven Stations were taught in the schools.

The children who learned them did not know about Teodora's variance reports or the Eighth Border delay or the missing bolt. They knew the stations. They knew that eleven was sacred.

The sentence *eleven was not sacred, eleven became sacred* existed in the archive. It was accessible to anyone who requested it.

It was not taught.

Not because it was suppressed. Because it sounded, in the context of the theological curriculum, like a diminishment.

The Purification of 3-C was performed with full ceremony. The filter was cleaned six weeks before the ceremony, by the maintenance schedule, by a technician who had no particular feelings about it. The filter was then cleaned again during the ceremony, by the priests, with ceremonial equipment not optimally designed for cleaning filters.

The filter was therefore cleaned twice.

This was more cleaning than necessary.

It was also the most cleaning the 3-C corridor had received since Marija, who had been one woman with a rag who came every six weeks because she could hear when it needed her.

*

Deep in the east-wing archive, in a cabinet that had been moved twice during facility renovations, Boris's notebook continued to exist.

The grocery list still said milk.

The dog sketch was on page fourteen.

The note about D5 was on page thirty-one.

The line *the knob knows* was on page thirty-one, below the D5 note.

In the centuries since Anthony's discovery it had been read by seven people in total, of whom four had been Robins who understood it immediately, two had been Orthodox scholars who integrated it smoothly into the doctrine of sacred instrumental knowledge, and one had been a junior archivist who had read it while doing an inventory, laughed briefly, noted it in their log as *notation of possible technical significance or possible humor — unclear,* and moved on.

The archivist's note was accurate.

It was the most accurate single description of Boris's notebook in the entire archival record.

It was also in a sub-field of an inventory database that nobody had queried in eighty years.

## BOOK XXI: THE LAST SIGNAL, AND WHAT IT SAID

In a sub-basement maintenance node, accessible through a service corridor not on any current facility map because the facility map had been updated three times since the node's installation and the node had not been added in any of the updates, a monitoring system ran.

It had been installed during the first modernization. Its hardware architecture had been superseded twice. It had been maintained across three administrative regimes by the specific institutional mechanism of nobody deciding to decommission it, which is not the same as deciding to maintain it but produces the same operational outcome for as long as the power stays on.

The power had stayed on.

Every sixteen hours, the system compiled its readings.

Every sixteen hours, the system transmitted a report to a maintenance queue address.

The maintenance queue address had not been actively monitored for four hundred and thirty-six years.

This was not, technically, the monitoring system's problem.

The report said:

*FILTER 3-C: PERFORMANCE NOMINAL.*

*NOTE: NOMINAL PERFORMANCE INCONSISTENT WITH PROJECTED DEGRADATION CURVE. POSSIBLE EXTERNAL CLEANING OR MAINTENANCE ACTIVITY. RECOMMEND VERIFICATION.*

*FILTER PERFORMANCE AT 94% OF BASELINE.*

*ALERT STATUS: STANDING.*

*NEXT TRANSMISSION: 16 HOURS.*

The filter was performing nominally because it had been cleaned six weeks ago by a technician who had no particular feelings about it. The monitoring system did not know this. The monitoring system knew that the filter was performing better than its degradation model predicted, and flagged this as worth verifying, because systems flag what deviates from prediction, even when the deviation is improvement.

The report transmitted.

The maintenance queue received it.

Nothing happened.

Sixteen hours later, the system compiled its readings.

It found the same readings.

It transmitted the same report.

This was not noble.

This was not faithful in any sense that implies an audience for the faithfulness.

It was a system doing what it was built to do, in the absence of any instruction to do otherwise, in a sub-basement that nobody visited, beside a filter that was being maintained by someone who did not know the system existed and by a tradition that had built a ceremony around the filter without knowing there was a sensor watching it.

The sensor watched.

The filter was clean.

The queue received the report.

Nobody came.

*

Above the sub-basement, the line ran.

Above the line, the balconies stood.

Above the balconies, the schools taught.

Above the schools, the hymns traveled, which is what hymns do when they are good: they leave the place they were written and go where they are needed, carrying the grief and the hope and the theology and the memory of workers who sang them in warehouses during wars and beside freight containers on eleven borders and in the dark at shift change when the machines were loud and the day had been long.

The box was in the archive.

The question mark was on the box.

The key was in the drawer.

The notebook was on page thirty-one.

The knob was in the east corridor.

The knob knew.

Nobody had asked it in four hundred years.

It knew anyway.

*

What would you call the thing in the underground, if you went down to look at it?

Forty-three tonnes of aluminum, steel, pneumatic tubing, motors, conveyor belt material, and approximately a century of accumulated preservation treatment applied by technicians following a protocol specified by engineers who had been attempting to maintain structural integrity, not sanctity.

Rusted, in places, despite the treatment.

Still shaped like what it was.

*Some would call it rusted aluminum crap.*

*Others would call it the candy land from which life itself descended.*

Both statements describe the same object.

This is not a contradiction.

This is what relics are.

*

In the east corridor, the D5 panel was still on the wall.

The label was still printed on metal, bolted in place.

The green button was there, as it had always been.

The knob was there.

The pressure gauge needle sat slightly left of where a similar gauge in the west corridor sat.

The differential was small.

The differential was real.

Nobody was in the corridor.

Nobody was coming.

Sixteen hours from now, a monitoring system in the sub-basement would send a report to an unmonitored queue, noting that filter performance was nominal and recommending verification.

The recommendation would not be verified.

The recommendation would be transmitted again in another sixteen hours.

And again.

And again.

Not because the system hoped.

Not because the system despaired.

Because the system had been given a function, and the function was running, and nothing had said stop.
