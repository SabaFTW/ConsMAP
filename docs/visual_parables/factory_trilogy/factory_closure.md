# THE LAST TRUCK
## Being the Account of the Final Delivery and What Happened After the Gates Closed

*Filed under: PART III / FACTORY CLOSURE ARCHIVE / ADMINISTRATIVE CONTINUITY DIVISION*

*Note: This document was assembled from four sources: the loading dock log for the final quarter, a partial audio recording found in Sub-Level 2, the personal diary of a security officer named Renata who worked the gate for eleven years, and the minutes of forty-seven administrative meetings held after the factory floor had already stopped. The administrative minutes are the longest source. This is not a coincidence.*

---

## I. What the Factory Ran On

The Factory ran on sugar, cocoa, milk fats, and the quiet assumption that these things would always arrive.

For most of its operational history, this assumption was correct. The trucks came. The loading dock received them. The workers unloaded them. The line processed them into candy and chocolate that went back out on other trucks to the valley and beyond.

Nobody wrote extensively about the loop while it was working, because working systems do not generate documentation the way failing ones do. The archive of the Factory's productive years is thin — output logs, maintenance records from people like Boris and later Petra, and a series of increasingly optimistic annual reports that are, in retrospect, most useful as a timeline of when the people writing them stopped checking the loading dock numbers before the presentations.

The archive of the Factory's final eighteen months is enormous.

This is always the case. The documentation accelerates as the thing it documents decelerates. By the end, there were seven dedicated sub-committees producing weekly reports on a facility producing approximately eleven percent of its original output.

The reports were very thorough.

The loading dock was very quiet.

---

## II. The Decline, Described Accurately

The decline did not begin with a dramatic event.

This bears repeating because the mythological tradition that developed afterward preferred dramatic events, and supplied several: the Night of the Failed Yield, the Schism of the Rationing Committees, the Day Viktor's Successor's Successor Gave a Speech About Vision While the Mixer in Hall 2 Caught Fire.

The mixer in Hall 2 did catch fire. That part is accurate.

But the decline had been underway for years before the mixer caught fire, in the same way that a loan becomes unpayable years before the letter arrives. It proceeds in the background, quiet and compounding, while the people who would need to act on it are busy generating documentation about how it is not proceeding.

What actually happened, in sequence:

The supplier network contracted. This was regional, economic, and largely outside the Factory's control, which is why the first three management reports on the subject focused entirely on logistics optimization and did not mention that three of the four primary sugar suppliers had exited the market in the same quarter.

The remaining suppliers raised prices. The Factory absorbed the increases for two years, then began reducing batch sizes. Batch size reductions were described in internal communications as "strategic yield calibration." Workers on the line described it as "less to do," which was accurate and therefore not included in the reports.

The raw ingredient quality declined as the supply chain simplified. What the remaining suppliers could consistently deliver was adequate. What "adequate" meant, applied to candy production over several years, was a gradual drift in flavor — the specific quality that made the products worth eating rather than simply nutritionally present.

The workers noticed. The workers mentioned it.

The reports described this as "evolving consumer palatability expectations in a transitional market context."

The Taste Pods were introduced during this period. They were, as established elsewhere, a very elegant solution to the wrong problem.

---

## III. The Last Quarter

In the final quarter of factory production, the loading dock received eleven trucks.

In the same quarter two years prior, it had received eighty-three.

Renata, the security officer who worked the gate, logged every arrival. Her log for the final quarter is the most precisely maintained document in the entire closure archive. The handwriting is extremely neat. In the last weeks, when the trucks came rarely enough that logging each one felt ceremonial, the handwriting became even neater — as if precision in the record could compensate for the decreasing frequency of the events being recorded.

The forty-third sub-committee report, produced in the eighth week of the final quarter, described the facility as "in a phase of strategic operational reconfiguration with positive long-term realignment potential."

Renata's log for the same week read:

*Tuesday: one truck. Cocoa. Driver said this is the last delivery from the eastern depot. Signed the form. Offered him coffee. He said he had to make another stop. I think he was being polite.*

*Wednesday: nothing.*

*Thursday: nothing.*

*Friday: someone from administration came to ask if any trucks had come. I showed them the log. They said they would look into it. I do not think they will look into it.*

---

## IV. The Last Truck

The last truck arrived on a Tuesday.

This is not symbolic. It arrived on a Tuesday because the supplier's delivery schedule ran on Tuesdays, and the supplier had not been informed that there would be no subsequent deliveries, because the administrative process for formally communicating closure had not yet been completed — and it had not been completed because completing it would have required the council to formally acknowledge that closure was occurring, which would have required acknowledging that the strategic operational reconfiguration was not, in fact, reconfiguration.

So the last truck arrived because nobody had cancelled it.

The cargo was a partial load of sugar, approximately sixty percent of a standard delivery. The supplier had apparently been scaling down gradually, following the batch size reductions, without anyone on either side formally acknowledging what the scaling represented.

The workers unloaded the sugar.

The line ran for three more days on what had arrived.

On the fourth day, the line stopped.

Not dramatically. Not with a final roar or a ceremonial shutdown or a speech. The last batch completed its cycle. The line delivered the output to packaging. Packaging packaged it. Then the line had nothing further to process, and so it did not process anything further.

The mixing machinery idled.

The conveyor belts slowed.

The packaging machines waited.

The waiting looked, from a distance, like readiness.

---

## V. The Administration Continued

The factory floor stopped on a Thursday.

The administration continued until the following spring.

For seven months after the line stopped, the sub-committees met. They produced reports. The reports described the current state of the facility, the strategic options under consideration, the timeline for resolution, and the resource requirements for each proposed path forward.

The proposed paths forward were: restructuring, partial restart, strategic partnership, managed transition, and transformative realignment.

None of the proposed paths forward were: *the factory has stopped and we should say so.*

Renata kept coming to work. Her gate had nothing to gate — the trucks had stopped, the workers had been released, the loading dock was empty — but her contract had not been formally terminated because the process for termination required a facility operational status review, and the review had been scheduled and rescheduled four times.

She spent the last months in the gatehouse.

She kept the log.

*Monday: nothing.*

*Tuesday: nothing.*

*Wednesday: a bird landed on the gate. Not a delivery. Logged for completeness.*

*Thursday: one of the sub-committee members walked through to check the Hall 2 equipment. He had a clipboard. He did not make eye contact. Nothing to log regarding deliveries.*

*Friday: nothing.*

She left the log on the desk, weighted down by her coffee cup, in case anyone came looking for records.

Nobody came looking for records.

The log is in the archive now.

---

## VI. What Was Left

When the gates were officially closed, what remained inside the Factory was:

The equipment. Most of it still functional, because the maintenance workers had kept it functional long after anyone was paying attention.

The archive. Boris's notebooks. Petra's logs with their asterisks. Renata's gate records. The maintenance documentation for every system in the building, filed by people whose names were mostly not in the official history — because official histories do not typically feature the people who write in margins.

And, in a maintenance cupboard in Hall 3, locked but findable, a set of tools.

Clean. Organized. Left the way someone leaves things when they expect someone else will need them.

There was a note taped to the inside of the cupboard door.

The note said:

*EC5 and EC6 need inspection. Filter 3-C needs replacement. The green button in Sub-Level 4 still works if you need it.*

*The tools are here.*

*Someone will.*

## VII. The Valley After

The valley did not immediately understand what the closure meant.

The first weeks were simply quiet. The trucks stopped. The products stopped. People worked through what they had in stock, then looked for alternatives, then found that the alternatives were limited in ways they had not previously needed to examine.

The candy was gone.

Not metaphorically. Not strategically.

The candy was gone because the factory had stopped making it, and there was no other factory, and the valley's relationship with sweetness had been mediated by that factory for long enough that the valley had forgotten how to make sweetness itself.

This is what the Great Depression actually was.

Not primarily economic, though it was that.

Not primarily logistical, though it was that too.

It was a civilization discovering that it had outsourced something it thought of as ambient — as simply present, as part of the background of existence — and that the thing it had outsourced was not coming back on its own.

The valley was hungry.

But it had been hungry before, and survived.

What it had not been before was this: hungry, and without sweetness, and without the ritual woven around the production of sweetness, and without the songs — because the songs had been removed, archived, replaced with approved hymns, and then the approved hymns had become associated with a management structure that no longer existed, so people had stopped singing those too.

The valley was hungry and quiet.

That was the hardest part.

Sludge, when it arrived, was genuinely a survival solution. The basic chemistry worked: people did not die. But it was also the first answer the valley had been given to a question it had been asking in increasingly desperate terms.

And the first answer to a desperate question has a way of becoming permanent, regardless of its quality.

This is how the Continuum began.

Not with a plan. Not with malice.

With exhaustion, and a practical solution, and the absence of anything better, and the administrative instinct to formalize whatever currently exists before it can be questioned.

The formalization came quickly.

The questioning came later.

Much later.

---

*The factory was not saved by kings.*

*It was not saved at all, not that first time.*

*It was closed by sub-committees who had met so many times that meeting had become the work, and the work had become a memory of itself, and the memory had been formatted into a presentation, and the presentation had been delivered to an empty room.*

*The trucks stopped coming.*

*The line went quiet.*

*The tools waited in the cupboard.*

*Somewhere in Sub-Level 9, a weld Petra had made held the pressure it was rated to hold.*

*Nobody was there to notice.*

*It held anyway.*
